## Part A

**1.** (a) Bean bag: weight mg downward (exerted by the Earth) and drag F_D upward (exerted by the air); nothing else. Cone: the same two forces, with drag much larger relative to weight. For each, taking x downward, m dv/dt = mg − F_D(v). Newton's first law (an unforced body keeps its velocity) means that *motion needs no force to continue*; a "force of motion" or "force of the throw" on a diagram is a force with no body exerting it, which Newton's third law forbids (every force is one side of an interaction between two bodies), and it double-counts the velocity, which already lives in the initial condition, not in the force balance. (b) Terminal speed is the speed at which the two arrows on the diagram are equal and opposite, so dv/dt = 0: mg = ½ρv_t²C_DA, v_t = √(2mg/(ρC_DA)). The Reynolds number Re = ρvd/μ decides the regime: for Re ≲ 1 drag is linear in v (Stokes, viscous), for Re ~ 10³–10⁵ the drag coefficient is roughly constant and drag is quadratic (inertial, a wake). A 4 cm ball at a few m/s has Re ≈ 1.225 × 3 × 0.04/1.81 × 10⁻⁵ ≈ 8 × 10³: quadratic. (c) The number is gh/v_t² (equivalently the ratio of the fall distance to the length 2m/(ρC_DA) over which drag removes a significant fraction of the momentum): the fractional lengthening of the fall time is ≈ gh/(6v_t²) to first order (question 5), and the video resolves about one frame in 20, 1.7%, over a 0.64 s fall at 30 fps (better with the frame-quantisation model, roughly 0.5% with ten repeats). Compute v_t from the mass, the frontal area and a tabulated C_D, then gh/(6v_t²): a bean bag (v_t ~ 20–30 m/s) gives 0.2–0.5%, invisible; a paper cone (v_t ~ 3–5 m/s) gives tens of percent and reaches terminal speed within the drop. (d) The cone's lateral drift and wobble (it is not falling along a line, so the point-particle model's "one vertical coordinate" fails: the aerodynamic force has a lateral component and a moment that the model has no state for), and its rotation or tumbling (a point has no orientation and no moment of inertia; a cone's drag depends on its attitude, so C_D is not a constant). A third: the cone's C_D is not even constant in time because the flow around it separates differently as it accelerates. *Proficient:* two-force diagrams with the bodies named, the no-"force-of-motion" argument from the first and third laws; v_t derived and Re ~ 10⁴ → quadratic; the gh/v_t² criterion with the two orders of magnitude; two named failures of the point model with the assumption each breaks.

**2.** (a) Specific energy ε = v²/2 − μ/r and specific angular momentum h = r × v. dε/dt = v·a + (μ/r²)ṙ; with a = −μr/r³ the first term is −(μ/r³)r·v = −(μ/r²)ṙ, so dε/dt = 0: energy is conserved because gravity is conservative (derivable from a potential). dh/dt = v × v + r × a = 0 + r × (−μr/r³) = 0: angular momentum is conserved because the force is central (parallel to r), whatever its magnitude. Kepler's second law (equal areas in equal times) is h = r²θ̇ = constant, i.e. dA/dt = h/2. (b) ε = v²/2 − μ/r = −μ/(2a) (the vis-viva relation, v² = μ(2/r − 1/a)). ε < 0: bound orbit (ellipse); ε = 0: parabolic, the escape condition, v = √(2μ/r); ε > 0: hyperbolic. (c) ε = −μ/(2a) decreases as a decreases, so drag, removing energy, *lowers* a; but v_c = √(μ/a) increases as a decreases, so the satellite ends up faster. The energy removed by drag is twice the kinetic energy gained: the potential energy falls by 2ΔE and the kinetic rises by ΔE (the virial relation for a 1/r potential, 2⟨K⟩ = −⟨U⟩). Drag acts mostly at perigee, where the air is densest and the speed highest; a retarding impulse at perigee lowers the *apogee* (the opposite side of the orbit), so the eccentricity falls and the orbit circularises before it decays. (d) Leapfrog preserves angular momentum exactly for a central force (the kick is along r, so r × Δv = 0 at every half step, up to round-off) and conserves a shadow energy, so the true energy oscillates with bounded amplitude and no secular drift; RK4 preserves neither exactly: its energy drifts monotonically (Phase 03 question 3) and its angular momentum drifts too. So an energy drift that grows linearly in an RK4 run tells you nothing about drag; in a leapfrog run a secular energy decrease that is absent from a drag-free control run, with the angular momentum still conserved to round-off, is physics. The control run without drag is the test. *Proficient:* both derivatives shown to vanish with the reason (conservative, central) and Kepler's second law identified; vis-viva with the sign meaning; "loses energy, lowers a, speeds up" with the potential-versus-kinetic bookkeeping and the apogee-lowering circularisation; the exact/approximate table for the two integrators and the control-run test.

**3.** (a) Molecules carry momentum; each elastic bounce off a wall transfers 2mv_x to it; the pressure is the momentum transferred per unit area per unit time, p = nm⟨v_x²⟩ = ⅓nm⟨v²⟩ (question 11). It is proportional to ⟨v²⟩, not ⟨v⟩, because the momentum per hit is ∝ v_x and the hit rate on the wall is also ∝ v_x: a faster molecule both hits harder and hits more often. Temperature measures the mean translational kinetic energy per molecule, ½m⟨v²⟩ = (3/2)kT, and equipartition says that every quadratic degree of freedom in the energy (each translational and rotational velocity component, each vibrational position and momentum) holds ½kT on average in equilibrium. (b) Sound is a pressure wave carried by molecular motion, so its speed must be set by the molecular speeds: a = √(γRT/M) against v_rms = √(3RT/M), ratio √(γ/3) = 0.683 for γ = 1.4. It is slower because the wave is carried by the *collective* motion along one direction and the compression is adiabatic (γ enters), while v_rms counts all three directions of random motion. (c) Assumptions: molecules are point-like (their own volume is negligible against the volume per molecule), they do not interact except in instantaneous elastic collisions (no intermolecular forces), and collisions are frequent enough that the gas is in local equilibrium (the mean free path is small against every length of interest). The first two fail at high density and low temperature (a gas bottle at 200 bar: the molecular volume and attractive forces give a compressibility factor of about 1.05–1.1 for air; a van der Waals or real-gas equation is needed); the third fails at low density: at 100 km the mean free path is about 0.14 m (question 11) and the Knudsen number Kn = λ/L for a 1 m vehicle is about 0.1, so the gas is no longer a continuum around the body and the "pressure on a surface" must be computed from individual molecular impacts (free-molecular or transitional flow), not from a continuum pressure field. (d) The ideal-gas law and hydrostatic equilibrium are physics (the first a model of the gas, the second Newton's second law for a fluid at rest); the temperature profile (288.15 K at sea level, −6.5 K/km, isothermal from 11 km) is a *convention*: an agreed average that no particular day follows. An altimeter converts pressure to altitude with the ISA profile; on a hot day the real atmosphere is less dense, pressure falls more slowly with height, and the altimeter reads *lower* than the true altitude ("from high to low, look out below" in reverse: hot air, true altitude above indicated). That is why altimeters are used for separation (all aircraft share the same error) and radar or GNSS for terrain clearance. *Proficient:* the momentum-transfer picture with the ⟨v²⟩ reason and the equipartition statement; √(γ/3) with the collective/adiabatic reason; three assumptions with their failure conditions and Kn ~ 0.1 at 100 km versus real-gas effects in a bottle; physics versus convention and the hot-day sign.

**4.** (a) KCL: the currents into a node sum to zero; it expresses conservation of charge together with the assumption that no charge accumulates at a node (quasi-static). KVL: the voltages around any closed loop sum to zero; it expresses conservation of energy for a charge carried round the loop, and it requires that the electric field be conservative, i.e. that there be no changing magnetic flux through the loop (Faraday's law, ∮E·dl = −dΦ_B/dt, violated when the flux changes) and that the circuit be small compared with the wavelength at the frequencies present, so that the field is the same everywhere in the circuit at a given instant (lumped-element assumption; a 1 m cable is "small" at 1 kHz and a transmission line at 1 GHz). (b) Faraday: a changing magnetic field induces a circulating electric field (∇ × E = −∂B/∂t); Ampère–Maxwell: a current *or a changing electric field* produces a circulating magnetic field (∇ × B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t). The displacement-current term closes the loop: a changing E makes B, whose change makes E, and so on; eliminating one field from the two curl equations in free space gives ∇²E = μ₀ε₀∂²E/∂t², a wave equation whose speed is 1/√(μ₀ε₀) = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s, which is why light was identified as an electromagnetic wave. (c) The Poynting vector S = E × B/μ₀ is the energy flux. In steady state the power crossing every sphere around the source is the same P (energy is conserved and none accumulates), so the average intensity on a sphere of radius r is P/(4πr²): inverse square is just the area of a sphere. A real antenna radiates the same total P but concentrates it: S(r, θ, φ) = P G(θ, φ)/(4πr²) with the gain G averaging to 1 over the sphere (for a lossless antenna); the 1/r² survives because energy conservation applies direction by direction in the far field. A half-wave dipole radiates a doughnut: maximum broadside (perpendicular to the wire), nulls along the wire's axis, azimuthally uniform, gain 1.64 (2.15 dBi) broadside. (d) A voltmeter is placed in parallel with the element and must draw as little current as possible, so its internal resistance is large (10 MΩ typical for a hand-held meter); an ammeter is placed in series and must drop as little voltage as possible, so its internal resistance ("burden") is small (fractions of an ohm, a shunt). Loading is the change the instrument causes in the quantity it measures by being part of the circuit. Decide in advance by comparing the meter's resistance with the circuit's: the voltmeter error is about R_source/(R_source + R_meter) where R_source is the Thévenin resistance seen at the measurement points, and the ammeter error about R_burden/R_circuit; if either ratio is comparable with the accuracy you need, change the method (question 12d). *Proficient:* charge and energy conservation named with the quasi-static and no-changing-flux conditions and Faraday as the violated equation; the two curl laws in words, the displacement current as the closing link and 1/√(μ₀ε₀); inverse square from the sphere with gain as a direction-dependent coefficient and the dipole doughnut with axial nulls; large/small internal resistances with the loading ratios.

**6.** (a) For the aircraft as a particle, the work–energy theorem gives d(½mV²)/dt = F_net·V. Along the flight path, with thrust along the velocity and lift perpendicular to it (the assumption: thrust aligned with the path, lift doing no work), the forces with components along V are T, −D and −mg sin γ (γ the climb angle), so m V dV/dt = (T − D)V − mgV sin γ = (T − D)V − mg dh/dt. Rearranging, (T − D)V = mg(dh/dt + (V/g) dV/dt) = mg d(h + V²/(2g))/dt. Define h_e = h + V²/(2g), the energy height (the altitude the aircraft would reach if all its kinetic energy were converted to potential energy); then P_s = (T − D)V/W = dh_e/dt, the specific excess power: excess thrust power per unit weight.
(b) P_s = (3000 − 1800) × 50.0/12 000 = 1200 × 50/12 000 = 5.00 m/s. At constant speed the whole P_s goes into dh/dt: rate of climb 5.00 m/s (300 m/min). (c) At constant h_e: Δh = (V₁² − V₂²)/(2g) = (3600 − 1600)/(2 × 9.81) = 2000/19.62 = 102 m. **7.** (a) Take the rocket of mass m moving at v; in dt it expels dm_e = ṁ dt of exhaust at speed v − v_e (ground frame). Momentum before: mv. After: (m − dm_e)(v + dv) + dm_e(v − v_e). With no external force these are equal: mv = mv + m dv − dm_e v − dm_e dv + dm_e v − dm_e v_e, so m dv = v_e dm_e (dropping the second-order dm_e dv). Since the rocket's mass changes by dm = −dm_e, m dv = −v_e dm, dv = −v_e dm/m, and integrating from m₀ to m_f: Δv = v_e ln(m₀/m_f). The rocket pushes against its own exhaust: the force on the rocket is the reaction to the force that accelerates the propellant backwards (Newton's third law), and the momentum of the exhaust exactly balances the momentum gained by the rocket; no external medium is involved, which is why rockets work *better* in vacuum (no back-pressure, no drag).
(b) Δv = 2200 ln(250/100) = 2200 × 0.9163 = 2016 m/s. Thrust F = ṁv_e = 7.50 × 2200 = 16 500 N. Burn time (250 − 100)/7.5 = 20.0 s. import numpy as np
import pytest
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
from scipy.special import ellipk


def pendulum_period(theta0: float, length: float, g: float = 9.81) -> float:
    """Period of a point pendulum released from rest at angle theta0 (rad).

    Integrates theta'' = -(g/L) sin(theta) and returns the time between two successive
    upward zero crossings of theta' (the far turning point, reached once per period).
    """
    def rhs(t, y):
        return [y[1], -(g / length) * np.sin(y[0])]

    def upward(t, y):
        return y[1]
    upward.direction = 1
    t_end = 10.0 * 2.0 * np.pi * np.sqrt(length / g)
    sol = solve_ivp(rhs, (0.0, t_end), [theta0, 0.0], events=upward, rtol=1e-11, atol=1e-12)
    crossings = sol.t_events[0]
    return float(crossings[1] - crossings[0])


def period_series(theta0: float, length: float, g: float = 9.81) -> float:
    """Small-amplitude series T0 (1 + theta0^2/16 + 11 theta0^4/3072)."""
    return 2.0 * np.pi * np.sqrt(length / g) * (1 + theta0**2 / 16 + 11 * theta0**4 / 3072)


def test_small_angle_limit():
    L, g = 1.0, 9.81
    assert pendulum_period(1e-3, L, g) == pytest.approx(2 * np.pi * np.sqrt(L / g), rel=1e-6)


def test_matches_elliptic_integral_at_60_degrees():
    L, g, th = 0.95, 9.81, np.radians(60)
    exact = 4 * np.sqrt(L / g) * ellipk(np.sin(th / 2) ** 2)   # ellipk takes m = k^2
    assert pendulum_period(th, L, g) == pytest.approx(exact, rel=1e-6)
    assert period_series(th, L, g) == pytest.approx(exact, rel=1e-3)   # series is 0.03% low at 60 deg


def test_energy_conserved_along_trajectory():
    L, g, th = 1.0, 9.81, np.radians(40)
    sol = solve_ivp(lambda t, y: [y[1], -(g / L) * np.sin(y[0])], (0, 20), [th, 0.0],
                    rtol=1e-11, atol=1e-12, dense_output=True)
    t = np.linspace(0, 20, 2001)
    y = sol.sol(t)
    E = 0.5 * L**2 * y[1] ** 2 + g * L * (1 - np.cos(y[0]))     # per unit mass
    assert np.max(np.abs(E - E[0])) / E[0] < 1e-8

(b) Hypotheses, each distinct, with what the fit says and the bench check: (i) **The ADC's reference is not the rail the multimeter measured.** On many boards the ADC reference is the regulator's nominal 3.30 V output or a separate reference pin, while the rail the learner probed sagged to 3.28 V under the board's load, or was probed on the far side of a filter or a diode. The fitted gain (3.301 V full scale) supports this directly. Check: measure the voltage on the board's reference/AREF pin or the ADC supply pin itself, with the board running; or read a known voltage (the board's own 3.3 V through a 2:1 divider, or a fresh alkaline cell measured by the meter) and compare counts. (ii) **ADC offset error** (a few counts, typical of a microcontroller SAR ADC, plus the ground potential difference between the sensor's ground and the ADC's ground through the breadboard's rail resistance). The fitted intercept of +2.5 counts supports it. Check: short the ADC input to ground (through 1 kΩ) and read the counts; then tie it to the reference and read full scale. (iii) **The multimeter's own gain error** (a 0.5% DC-volts specification is common on inexpensive meters). It would produce a gain discrepancy but not a counts offset, so it cannot explain the intercept alone; it could explain part of the 0.6% gain. Check: measure the same voltage with a second meter, or measure a reference whose value is known independently (the board's reference IC, a precision reference, or, for 0.1%, a calibrated meter borrowed from a club). (iv) **Source impedance.** The divider's Thévenin resistance is 10 kΩ ∥ R_th, up to 5 kΩ at mid-range; a SAR ADC's sampling capacitor needs the source to charge it within the acquisition time, and a source above the datasheet's limit (typically 10 kΩ or so) reads low by an amount that *varies with R_th*, i.e. nonlinearly with V. The residuals are random at ±1 count with no bowing, so this is ruled out at the 0.1% level (and 5 kΩ is within spec for most boards). Check: add a 100 nF capacitor from the node to ground (a charge reservoir) and see whether the counts change. (v) **Thermistor self-heating** changes R_th and the node voltage, but both instruments see the same node at the same moment, so it cancels in this comparison; it would appear only as a temperature error (check by comparing with a reference thermometer, or by halving the divider current with a 20 kΩ resistor and seeing whether the inferred temperature moves). (vi) **Rail drift between readings** (the meter reading and the ADC reading not taken simultaneously while the USB supply wanders): would produce scatter, not a clean gain-plus-offset line; the ±1 count residuals rule it out. So (i) and (ii) survive and together explain the table; the one-line fix is to convert with the reference the ADC actually uses, measured at its pin, and to subtract the measured zero offset, or, better, to make the measurement *ratiometric* (see c).
(c) At 1.603 V with V_ref = 3.30 V: R_th = 10.0 kΩ × (3.30/1.603 − 1) = 10.59 kΩ; T = 1/(1/298.15 + ln(10.586/10.0)/3950) = 1/(3.3540 × 10⁻³ + 1.443 × 10⁻⁵) = 296.87 K = 23.7 °C. With V 0.6% high (1.6126 V): R_th = 10.46 kΩ, T = 24.0 °C: ΔT = 0.26 °C (the node sensitivity is about 37 mV/K here, so 9.6 mV of gain error is 0.26 K). For a ±1 °C requirement it is a quarter of the budget, and the thermistor's own ±1% resistance tolerance (≈ ±0.25 °C) and the β tolerance (±1%, ≈ ±0.1 °C at 24 °C, growing away from 25 °C) are of the same size, so yes: fix it, because it is free. The right fix is not a better number for V_ref but a ratiometric computation: if the divider is powered from the same rail the ADC uses as its reference, then counts/1023 = R_fix/(R_th + R_fix) exactly, and R_th = R_fix(1023/counts − 1) with no reference voltage in it at all; with the 2.5-count offset removed, 498 counts gives R_th = 10.65 kΩ and 23.6 °C. The β model assumes a single activation energy (ln R linear in 1/T); real NTC thermistors deviate by about ±0.5–1 °C over 0–50 °C and several degrees over −40 to +125 °C, where the three-term Steinhart–Hart fit or the manufacturer's R–T table is used.
(d) (i) A 12 V wall adapter violates two hard limits: supply ≤ 9 V DC, and battery or USB power only (an adapter is mains-derived; even a well-made one brings mains-connected equipment to the bench, which the kit excludes). An LED without a series resistor violates "every LED through a resistor": the diode's exponential I–V (question 14d) lets the 12 V source push whatever current its wiring allows, the LED overheats and fails within a second, possibly with a hot fragment; on a USB supply the port's current limit or polyfuse trips instead. Safe: stay on USB 5 V and use a 150 Ω resistor for 20 mA (near the LED's rating; 3 V across 150 Ω), or add LEDs in parallel each with its own resistor; brightness beyond the rating is not available safely. (ii) A multimeter in the current range is a near-short (a 0.1 Ω shunt, question 12d). Across the rail it short-circuits the supply: a USB port delivers its limit (0.5–3 A) and shuts down or the polyfuse trips, a 9 V battery delivers an amp or more and gets hot, and the meter's current fuse blows (the SparkFun multimeter tutorial has a section on changing it for exactly this reason). Current is measured *in series*: break the rail at one point and insert the meter, or measure the voltage across a known small series resistor (1 Ω: 1 mV per mA) and apply Ohm's law, which also avoids the burden error. (iii) The 9 V limit is on the *supply*; an input pin's absolute maximum is V_dd + 0.3 V ≈ 3.6 V on a 3.3 V board (5.3 V on a 5 V board), and 9 V on it drives the pin's protection diode into the supply rail, heating the die and often destroying the pin or the chip, or back-feeding 9 V into the 3.3 V rail and everything on it. Safe: a resistive divider that keeps the pin below 3.3 V under *all* conditions (a fresh 9 V battery reads 9.6 V, so 3:1 giving 3.2 V max, or 4:1 for margin), with the lower resistor small enough for the ADC's source-impedance limit, and preferably a series resistor into the pin so that the protection diode current is limited if the divider is wrong. The report must contain the completed safety box (power source and voltage, maximum current, no mains, no lithium charging, capacitors within limits and discharged, soldering safety if any), a schematic and a photograph of the breadboard, the raw readings (both instruments, with time stamps and which was read first), the meter's specification used for its uncertainty, the ADC's reference and offset measurement, the uncertainty on every converted value, and the statement of which numbers are measured and which are computed from the β model; without the safety box it is not graded. *Proficient:* the five converted values with the sign pattern, the two-point gain and offset, 3.30 V and ≈ 2.5 counts; at least four distinct hypotheses, the reference and the offset identified as supported by the fit, source impedance and drift ruled out by the residuals, each with a bench check; 23.7 °C, ΔT = 0.26 °C, "fix it" with the ratiometric reason and the β model's range; for each of the three proposals the specific limit, the failure mechanism and the safe method, and the report's hard requirements.
