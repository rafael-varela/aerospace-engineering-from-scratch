## 1. Your own bottle, predicted (s = 15: D = 95 mm, d = 4.25 mm)

(a) π × 0.095²/4 × (0.180 + 0.030) = **1.49 L**: **yes**, within the 2 L limit (with a little to spare for the manometer's 20 mL; the repeat runs reuse the same water, caught in a clean basin and poured back; fresh water for each run would take about 4.5 L and break the limit).

(b) **T = 92.9 ± 6.4 s (6.9 %)**. Budget: C_d 4.57 s, hole 4.37 s, bottle 0.98 s, level readings 0.22 s and 0.53 s. C_d and the hole dominate almost equally; the hole term is 2 × 0.1/4.25 = 4.7 %.

(c) Re_jet = **7960** at 180 mm and **3250** at 30 mm.

**Check.** T scales as D²/d²: 102.7 s × (95/90)² × (4.0/4.25)² × (√0.18 − √0.03)/(√0.2 − √0.03) = 92.9 s.

**Common mistakes.** Measuring h from the base instead of the hole centre; using the bottle's outside diameter.

## 2. Derivations

(a) Bernoulli from the free surface (pressure p_a, speed V_s, height h above the hole) to the vena contracta (p_a, V_j): V_j² − V_s² = 2gh. Continuity: A V_s = C_d a V_j (C_d a is the effective jet area times the velocity coefficient). With r = C_d a/A: V_j² (1 − r²) = 2gh. The level falls at V_s = dh/dt (downward): A dh/dt = −C_d a V_j = −C_d a √(2gh/(1 − r²)). Divide by 2√h: d√h/dt = −C_d (a/A)√(g/2)/√(1 − r²), so **k → k/√(1 − r²)**. For the lesson's bottle r = 0.61 × (4/90)² = 1.2 × 10⁻³ and the correction is r²/2 = **7 × 10⁻⁷**: negligible.

(b) The jet contracts after leaving a sharp hole because fluid approaching along the wall cannot turn through 90° instantly: its area is C_c a. Friction in the short hole slows it slightly: V_j = C_v√(2gh). The flow rate is the product, Q = (C_c a)(C_v√(2gh)), so C_d = C_c C_v (about 0.62 × 0.98). A rounded entry lets the flow turn gradually, the contraction disappears (C_c → 1), and C_d rises towards C_v, about 0.9–0.98.

(c) Both legs lie along a board at angle θ. A meniscus that moves a distance s along the tube rises s sin θ. The along-tube separation L of the two menisci (after subtracting the zero) corresponds to a vertical difference **Δh = L sin θ**. Then V = √(2(ρ_w − ρ_a) g L sin θ/ρ_a): ∂ln V/∂L = 1/(2L), ∂ln V/∂θ = cot θ/2, ∂ln V/∂ρ_a ≈ −1/(2ρ_a). So **(u_V/V)² = ¼[(u_L/L)² + (u_θ/tan θ)² + (u_ρ/ρ_a)²]**, with u_θ in radians.

(d) A liquid surface curved with radii R₁, R₂ has a pressure jump σ(1/R₁ + 1/R₂). For a cylindrical jet of radius d/2 that is 2σ/d; for a hemispherical drop forming at the hole, 4σ/d. With σ = 0.073 N/m and d = 4 mm: 36.5 Pa and 73 Pa, heads of **3.7 mm** and **7.5 mm** of water. Below about this head the jet cannot form; the fit must stop well above it.

**Common mistakes.** In (c), using Δh = L/sin θ (inverted); in (a), forgetting that the level speed is downward.

## 4. A wind-tunnel contraction and an aeroplane's pitot (s = 15: CR = 7, L = 55 mm)

(a) Continuity: V_sc = V_ts/CR. Bernoulli between the wall taps (same streamline region, no losses in a good contraction): p_sc − p_ts = ½ρ_a(V_ts² − V_sc²) = ½ρ_aV_ts²(1 − 1/CR²), so **V_ts = √(2Δp/(ρ_a(1 − 1/CR²)))**. Δh = 55 sin 15° = 14.24 mm, Δp = (998.2 − 1.196) × 9.81 × 0.01424 = 139.2 Pa, ρ_a = 1.196 kg/m³: **V_ts = 15.42 ± 0.28 m/s** (1.8 %; the factor 1 − 1/CR² is exact and adds no uncertainty). Ignoring the settling-chamber velocity gives 15.26 m/s, **1.0 % low**: small at CR = 7, 3.3 % at CR = 4.

(b) ISA 3000 m: ρ = 0.9091 kg/m³; Δp = ½ × 0.9091 × 60² = 1636 Pa; **Δh = 167 mm** of water. On the ground a water U-tube is a primary standard for exactly that pressure: apply a known Δh to the pitot and static ports with a hand-pumped (gently, by the manufacturer's test set, not by mouth) or syringe-driven source and read the airspeed indicator against V = √(2Δp/ρ₀) (an indicated airspeed is calibrated with ρ₀ = 1.225 kg/m³, `P08.04`). In flight it is useless: it responds slowly, it spills under acceleration and attitude changes, and 167 mm of water column is a large instrument. On a real aircraft this check is carried out only by certified maintenance personnel with approved pitot-static test equipment, never as a home experiment.

**Common mistakes.** Using the room's density in the aeroplane case; forgetting sin θ.

## 5. Reading a 3 m/s fan to 5 %

Δh = 0.550 mm at 3 m/s (ρ_a = 1.196 kg/m³).

(a) u_L = 0.6 mm along the tube, angle to 0.5°: **54.5 %** upright, **27.3 %** at 30°, **9.8 %** at 10°, **6.9 %** at 5.3°. With the angle to 0.1°: **5.1 %** at 5.3° and **9.5 %** at 10° (at 10° the reading dominates, at 5.3° the angle).

(b) Upright, one reading gives 54.5 %; for 10 % with independent readings you need (54.5/10)² ≈ **30** readings. Doubtful: a 1 mm scale read to "0.3 mm" is partly quantisation and partly a systematic habit (parallax, which side of the meniscus), neither of which averages away; 30 readings of a quantity smaller than one graduation mostly measure your judgement.

(c) Tilt to about 4–5° and measure the angle to 0.1° or better: on a board 500 mm long, the rise measured to 0.5 mm gives the angle to 0.06°. At 4° with u_θ = 0.1° the airspeed uncertainty is 4.0 %. Alternatively keep 10° and halve the reading uncertainty (0.15 mm per meniscus, by photographing a finer scale beside the tube): 4.8 %. Either way the improvement is in a measurement (angle or reading), not in averaging.

## 6. What does the Reynolds number tell you here?

(a) Not necessarily. Sharp-orifice C_d is nearly independent of Re above a few thousand; at Re of several hundred to two thousand it depends on Re and on the edge (viscous effects in the short passage and on the contraction). Measure it: a second bottle with a 1 mm (or 2 mm) hole, same procedure, C_d against Re over the run. With a 1 mm hole, the hole diameter is even harder to measure, and the hole term dominates even more.

(b) The 6 mm straw (Re ≈ 1200): well above the range where viscous effects make a pitot probe read high (they matter below a probe Reynolds number of order 100). The 1 mm tube at Re ≈ 200 is close to that range and would also respond more slowly.

(c) It does not matter directly: it tells you the fan's flow is turbulent, as you would expect. What matters is the jet's unsteadiness (the reading fluctuates; the water column's slow response averages it) and its decay with distance from the fan (so "the airspeed" depends on where the straw is). The procedure fixed the distance (300 mm, on the axis), waited for the column to settle, and repeated each reading.

## Check-yourself answers

1. A dh/dt = −C_d a √(2gh). The outflow is proportional to √h, so d√h/dt = (1/(2√h)) dh/dt is constant: √h falls linearly.
2. The contraction of the jet (C_c, about 0.62) and the slight loss of speed by friction (C_v, about 0.98); C_d ≈ 0.60–0.62 for a sharp-edged round hole (typical).
3. The hole diameter: the flow rate goes as d², so a 2.5 % uncertainty in d is a 5 % uncertainty in C_d.
4. A free jet cannot sustain a pressure difference with the still air round it, so its static pressure equals the room's.
5. ½ × 1.2 × 25 = 15 Pa; Δh = 15/(998 × 9.81) = 1.53 mm.
6. The same vertical head moves the menisci 1/sin θ further along the tube while the reading uncertainty stays the same; but the angle's uncertainty enters as u_θ/tan θ, which grows as θ shrinks.
7. All three runs share the hole and bottle diameters, whose uncertainty (5 %) dominates; the scatter shows only the timing and level noise. Report the mean with one run's uncertainty (0.632 ± 0.032), not ± 0.0007.
8. So that the comparison is a test: a prediction written after seeing the data is fitted to it, and a predicted resolution limit written afterwards cannot show whether you understood your instrument.
