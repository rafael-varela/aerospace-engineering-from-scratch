## Part A

**1.** (a) A fluid deforms continuously under any shear stress, however small; a solid takes a finite deformation and stops. The continuum hypothesis treats density, velocity and stress as smooth fields defined at every point, which needs the smallest length of interest to be much larger than the molecular mean free path λ. Knudsen number Kn = λ/L: wing at 3000 m, 9 × 10⁻⁸/1.5 ≈ 6 × 10⁻⁸, deep in the continuum; sounding rocket at 100 km, 0.1/0.1 ≈ 1, transitional to free-molecular flow, where the Navier–Stokes equations with no-slip fail (slip and rarefied-gas methods take over). This phase describes the first. (b) The rate of change of a property B of the system that occupies a control volume at an instant equals the rate of change of B inside the fixed volume plus the net outflow of B through its surface: dB_sys/dt = d/dt∫_CV ρb dV + ∮_CS ρb(u·n) dA, with b = B per unit mass. Newton's second law and conservation of mass and energy are statements about a fixed mass; the theorem rewrites them for a region of space through which the fluid flows, which is what an engine or a nozzle is. Differential continuity: ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρu) = 0; for incompressible flow (Dρ/Dt = 0) ∇·u = 0. (c) Hydrostatic: σ_ij = −pδ_ij with no motion (lesson 01, and the manometer of lesson 19); fails as soon as the fluid moves relative to itself (a manometer read while the water is still sloshing). Inviscid: σ_ij = −pδ_ij in a moving fluid (Euler's equation, Bernoulli, potential flow, lessons 04, 08, 09, 17); fails in boundary layers and wakes, where it predicts zero drag. Newtonian laminar: σ_ij = −pδ_ij + μ(∂u_i/∂x_j + ∂u_j/∂x_i) (lessons 06, 07 laminar, 10, 11, 12, 18); fails in turbulent flow at the scales that are not resolved, and for non-Newtonian fluids (gelled propellants, blood). Turbulence model: the mean stress plus a modelled Reynolds stress −ρ⟨u_i'u_j'⟩, for example 2μ_tS_ij − (2/3)ρkδ_ij (lessons 14, 15, and the correlations of 07 and 16); fails in strongly separated, swirling or curved flows and in transition. (d) Lagrangian: follow fluid particles, properties as functions of the particle label and time. Eulerian: properties as fields over fixed points and time. D/Dt is the rate of change following a particle: the local change at a point plus the change from being carried to a point with a different value. Streamline: tangent to the velocity at one instant. Pathline: the trajectory of one particle. Streakline: the locus at one instant of all particles that passed through one point. A smoke wire (or a dye injector) shows streaklines; a tracked balloon traces a pathline. In steady flow all three coincide. *Proficient:* the shear definition and Kn with both numbers and the verdict; RTT in words and symbols with the fixed-mass argument, ∇·u = 0; the four stress models each with a tensor, a lesson and a failure; the three lines defined, with smoke-wire = streakline, balloon = pathline, steady = coincide.

**2.** (a) Euler's equation ρDu/Dt = −∇p + ρg, projected on a streamline (coordinate s) for steady flow: ρu du/ds = −dp/ds − ρg dz/ds. Integrated with ρ constant: p + ½ρu² + ρgz = constant along the streamline. Assumptions: steady, inviscid (no shear stress), incompressible, along one streamline, no shaft work or heat addition, gravity the only body force. If the flow is also irrotational, the constant is the same on every streamline, so Bernoulli holds between any two points; that needs ∇ × u = 0, which a flow starting from a uniform stream keeps outside boundary layers and wakes (Kelvin). Failures: across a fan the shaft adds energy (no shaft work fails); in a pipe with friction the stress is not zero (inviscid fails, a head loss appears); in a pitot at M = 0.8 the density changes by 35% (incompressible fails; question 21); in a separated wake the total pressure is lost to turbulence (inviscid and irrotational both fail). (b) ω = ∇ × u; Γ = ∮_C u·dl. Stokes: Γ = ∫_S ω·n dA over any surface bounded by C. Kelvin: the circulation round a closed material loop is constant in time if the fluid is inviscid, the density is a function of pressure alone (barotropic) and the body forces are conservative. Before the start the air is at rest and the circulation round a large material loop enclosing the wing is zero. The loop is far from the wing, where viscosity is negligible, so it stays zero. When the wing develops a bound circulation Γ (by the Kutta condition at its sharp trailing edge), a vortex of −Γ must be inside the same loop: the starting vortex, shed from the trailing edge and left behind. (c) Kutta–Joukowski: the lift per unit span on any 2-D body in a uniform stream U with circulation Γ is ρUΓ, perpendicular to U. D'Alembert: the drag on any body in steady, inviscid, irrotational flow is zero. Both hold at once because the force from potential flow is only the circulation term: the pressure distribution is fore–aft symmetric, so it gives no drag, but it is not top–bottom symmetric if Γ ≠ 0, so it gives lift. Real flows have viscosity, so they have boundary layers that separate on bluff bodies and leave a low-pressure wake (pressure drag), and wall shear (friction drag). Potential flow has a one-parameter family of solutions (any Γ). The Kutta condition (the flow leaves a sharp trailing edge smoothly) picks the one that a real viscous flow selects. (d) The free vortex has ω_z = (1/r)d(rv_θ)/dr = (1/r)d(Γ/2π)/dr = 0 for r > 0: the particles go round the axis but do not spin about their own centres (a small cross of sticks keeps its orientation). All the vorticity is concentrated on the axis (a delta function of strength Γ). In a real trailing vortex the vorticity sits in a viscous core of finite radius. It was generated at the wing's surface, as boundary-layer vorticity, shed at the trailing edge as a vortex sheet that rolls up into the two tip vortices, and it then diffuses outward (question 13(c)). *Proficient:* the derivation with all six assumptions and the irrotational extension; four failures each tied to its assumption; ω, Γ, Stokes, Kelvin's three conditions and the large-loop argument for the starting vortex; KJ and d'Alembert reconciled by symmetry, separation and friction named, the Kutta condition choosing Γ; ω = 0 for r > 0 with the shed-sheet origin of real vorticity.

**3.** (a) At high Re the viscous term is small compared with inertia everywhere except in a thin layer next to the wall, where the no-slip condition forces a velocity gradient large enough for viscous stress to matter. Balancing u∂u/∂x ~ U²/x against ν∂²u/∂y² ~ νU/δ² gives δ ~ √(νx/U), δ/x ~ Re_x^{−1/2}. Prandtl's equations keep the streamwise convection and the wall-normal viscous diffusion ∂²u/∂y², drop ∂²u/∂x², and reduce the y-momentum equation to ∂p/∂y = 0: the pressure across the layer is the one the outer inviscid flow imposes, p(x) with dp/dx = −ρU_e dU_e/dx. δ₉₉: the height where u = 0.99U_e. δ* = ∫(1 − u/U_e)dy, the displacement thickness: the distance the outer streamlines are pushed away from the wall by the velocity deficit. A wind-tunnel designer diverges the test-section walls by δ* along the tunnel so that the core flow does not accelerate. θ = ∫(u/U_e)(1 − u/U_e)dy, the momentum thickness, measures the momentum deficit and hence the drag. (b) An adverse gradient (dp/dx > 0) decelerates the fluid. The near-wall fluid has the least momentum, so it slows most, until the wall shear ∂u/∂y|₀ falls to zero (separation) and the flow reverses beneath a detached shear layer. A turbulent layer mixes high-momentum fluid toward the wall, so it has a fuller profile and survives a larger pressure rise. The momentum integral relates dθ/dx to τ_w, θ, H and dU_e/dx; Thwaites observed that for laminar layers θ²/ν·dU_e/dx (the parameter λ) nearly fixes the profile shape. That makes the momentum integral a single ODE with a closed-form solution from U_e(x) alone, and separation occurs at λ ≈ −0.09. (c) In a quiet stream the laminar layer becomes linearly unstable to two-dimensional travelling waves (Tollmien–Schlichting waves) above a critical Re_δ*. These are amplified downstream and become three-dimensional, form Λ-structures and turbulent spots, which merge into a turbulent layer. Orr–Sommerfeld: the Navier–Stokes equations linearised about a steady parallel flow U(y), with a normal-mode disturbance v = φ(y)e^{iα(x − ct)}; it gives the complex wave speed c for each α and Re. The critical Reynolds number is the lowest Re at which any mode grows (c_i > 0). It is where growth *begins*; it does not mean transition there, and below it a flow can still become turbulent through finite disturbances. e^N: integrate the growth rate of the most amplified frequency along the surface to get ln(A/A₀) = N; transition is predicted where N reaches an empirical value, about 9 for a quiet free stream (an amplification of e⁹ ≈ 8000), calibrated against flight and quiet-tunnel data. (d) Bypass transition by high free-stream turbulence (a propeller slipstream over the wing) skips the TS route and moves transition forward. Roughness and contamination (insect remains, rain, rivet heads, a gap) trip the layer at or just behind the roughness. Others: crossflow instability on a swept wing; attachment-line contamination; a laminar separation bubble that transitions in its free shear layer. *Proficient:* the δ ~ √(νx/U) balance and the dropped terms with ∂p/∂y = 0; δ*, θ defined with the wall-divergence use; separation as zero wall shear with the turbulent-mixing reason and λ fixing the profile; TS route, Orr–Sommerfeld's assumptions, "growth begins, not transition", N = 9 as an amplification; two other routes with their effect.

**4.** (a) u = ū + u', with the mean of u' zero. Average the x-momentum equation written in conservation form, ∂u/∂t + ∂(u_ju)/∂x_j = −(1/ρ)∂p/∂x + ν∇²u. The product u_ju averages to ū_jū + ⟨u_j'u'⟩, so the mean equation acquires −∂⟨u_j'u'⟩/∂x_j, a divergence of an extra stress −ρ⟨u_i'u_j'⟩, the Reynolds stresses. In 3-D: four mean equations (continuity and three momentum), unknowns ū, v̄, w̄, p̄ and six independent Reynolds stresses: ten unknowns, four equations. Equations for the stresses contain new triple correlations, and so on: that is the closure problem. Boussinesq: −ρ⟨u_i'u_j'⟩ = 2μ_tS̄_ij − (2/3)ρkδ_ij, a scalar eddy viscosity. It aligns the stress with the mean strain and makes the normal stresses equal in a simple shear. It cannot represent anisotropy (secondary flow in a square duct), stress that lags the strain (rapidly changing or separating flow), or the effect of streamline curvature and rotation. (b) Kolmogorov: at high enough Re the small scales are statistically isotropic and depend only on ν and ε; in an inertial range between the energy-containing and dissipative scales they depend on ε alone. Scales: η = (ν³/ε)^{1/4}, τ_η = (ν/ε)^{1/2}, v_η = (νε)^{1/4} (with v_ηη/ν = 1). In the inertial range E(k) = C ε^{2/3}k^{−5/3} for 1/L ≪ k ≪ 1/η. Taylor's hypothesis: the eddies are carried past a probe faster than they evolve, so the time series at the probe is a spatial cut, x = Ut. It requires u'/U small (a few percent) and fails in separated flows, near walls and in jets with large intensity. (c) If a physical relation involves n variables and the dimension matrix has rank r, it can be written as a relation between n − r dimensionless groups. Rank, not the number of dimensions, because the dimensions may not be independent for the given variables (a problem with M, L and T in which only combinations like L/T appear has rank 2). For the aeroplane, Re ∝ ρVc/μ, so a 1:5 model in the same air needs five times the speed: V ≈ 300 m/s, transonic (question 9: 242 m/s, M = 0.71), while the aeroplane flies at M = 0.18. Both cannot match in the same atmosphere. At cruise M = 0.18 the compressibility effect on drag is below 1% (question 21), while the Reynolds number sets transition and skin friction (questions 16 and 18), so Re matters more. (d) The rule bounds the density change of an isentropic deceleration to rest: ρ₀/ρ − 1 = (1 + 0.2M²)^{2.5} − 1 = 4.6% at M = 0.3 (question 21), and the error in airspeed from incompressible Bernoulli to 1.1%. It is about the flow, because the density change depends on the speed relative to the speed of sound; the same air is incompressible in a wind-tunnel settling chamber and compressible in its test section. *Proficient:* the averaged product with the Reynolds stress, 4 equations and 10 unknowns, Boussinesq with what it misses; Kolmogorov's hypotheses with the three scales, the −5/3 range, Taylor with its condition; n − r with a reason, the 1:5 impossibility with Re as the one that matters at M = 0.18; 4.6% density, 1.1% speed, flow not gas.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq
from scipy.integrate import solve_bvp


def _rhs(y):
    """Blasius f''' + f f''/2 = 0 as a first-order system y = (f, f', f'')."""
    return np.array([y[1], y[2], -0.5 * y[0] * y[2]])


def _rk4(s, eta_max, n):
    """Integrate from eta = 0 with f(0) = f'(0) = 0, f''(0) = s by classical RK4 on n steps."""
    h = eta_max / n
    y = np.empty((n + 1, 3))
    y[0] = (0.0, 0.0, s)
    for k in range(n):
        k1 = _rhs(y[k])
        k2 = _rhs(y[k] + 0.5 * h * k1)
        k3 = _rhs(y[k] + 0.5 * h * k2)
        k4 = _rhs(y[k] + h * k3)
        y[k + 1] = y[k] + h * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4) / 6
    return np.linspace(0.0, eta_max, n + 1), y


def blasius_shoot(eta_max=10.0, n=1000):
    """Blasius solution by shooting: find s = f''(0) so that f'(eta_max) = 1.

    Returns (s, eta, y) with y[:, 0..2] = f, f', f''.
    """
    miss = lambda s: _rk4(s, eta_max, n)[1][-1, 1] - 1.0
    s = brentq(miss, 0.1, 1.0, xtol=1e-15, rtol=1e-15)
    eta, y = _rk4(s, eta_max, n)
    return s, eta, y


def thicknesses(eta, y):
    """Displacement and momentum thickness coefficients (in units of sqrt(nu x / U)) and H."""
    fp = y[:, 1]
    d_star = eta[-1] - y[-1, 0]                      # integral of (1 - f') = eta_max - f(eta_max) exactly
    w = np.full(eta.size, 2.0); w[1:-1:2] = 4.0; w[0] = w[-1] = 1.0
    theta = (eta[1] - eta[0]) / 3 * np.sum(w * fp * (1 - fp))   # Simpson; n must be even
    return d_star, theta, d_star / theta


def blasius_bvp(eta_max=10.0):
    """Independent route: scipy.integrate.solve_bvp on the same problem."""
    e = np.linspace(0, eta_max, 201)
    guess = np.vstack([e - 1 + np.exp(-e), 1 - np.exp(-e), np.exp(-e)])
    sol = solve_bvp(lambda x, y: np.vstack([y[1], y[2], -0.5 * y[0] * y[2]]),
                    lambda a, b: np.array([a[0], a[1], b[1] - 1.0]), e, guess, tol=1e-10, max_nodes=100000)
    assert sol.success
    return sol.sol(0.0)[2]
```
