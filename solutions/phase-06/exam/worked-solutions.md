## Part A

**1.** (a) An inertial frame is one in which a free particle moves in a straight line at constant speed; Newton's second law equates the net force to the acceleration measured in such a frame, so in any frame that accelerates or rotates relative to it the measured acceleration differs by the frame's own acceleration terms, and F = ma fails unless they are added. A runway frame rotates with the Earth at Ω = 7.29 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s. At 45° N and 250 m/s the extra terms are of order 2Ωv ≈ 0.036 m/s² (Coriolis; its horizontal part 2Ωv sin 45° = 0.026 m/s²) and Ω²R cos 45° = 0.024 m/s² (centrifugal, normally absorbed into "effective g"); the Euler term α × r is zero because Ω is constant, and the acceleration of the Earth's centre (about 0.006 m/s² toward the Sun) is cancelled by the Sun's gravity on the body. For an arrested landing these are below 10⁻³ of the 37 m/s² deceleration over 2.6 s: negligible. For a 60 s artillery flight they integrate to about 130 m of drift (question 8), and a Foucault pendulum *is* the Coriolis term: there is nothing else to see. (b) Polar: v = ṙ e_r + rθ̇ e_θ; a = (r̈ − rθ̇²) e_r + (rθ̈ + 2ṙθ̇) e_θ, with −rθ̇² the centripetal term and 2ṙθ̇ the Coriolis term of polar coordinates (the basis vectors rotate). Path: v = V e_t; a = V̇ e_t + (V²/ρ) e_n, the tangential (speed change) and normal (direction change) terms, ρ the radius of curvature. A radar measures range, azimuth and their rates (polar about the radar); a pilot feels the path-coordinate components (V̇ along the flight path and V²/ρ normal to it, the load factor). r̈ is the rate of change of the range, which depends on where the radar is; V̇ is the rate of change of speed, which does not. They coincide only when the aircraft flies straight toward or away from the radar (question 5: r̈ = 1.5 m/s² while V̇ = −5.38 m/s²). (c) Transport theorem: (dA/dt)_inertial = (dA/dt)_rotating + ω × A. For the position of a point P, r = r_O + ρ with O the moving origin: v = v_O + v_rel + ω × ρ. Differentiate again, applying the theorem to v_rel and to ω × ρ: a = a_O + a_rel + α × ρ + ω × (ω × ρ) + 2ω × v_rel. Terms: a_O, the origin's acceleration (a ship's heave under a landing helicopter); a_rel, as seen in the moving frame; α × ρ, the Euler term (a spinning-up centrifuge or a rotor in a manoeuvre); ω × (ω × ρ), centripetal (a centrifuge, the Earth's effective-g correction, a helicopter blade's tension); 2ω × v_rel, Coriolis (artillery drift, the Foucault pendulum, a fuel slosh or a crew member walking in a spinning station, lead–lag forcing on a flapping rotor blade). (d) In a runway-fixed (inertial enough) frame there is no centrifugal force: the only horizontal force is the horizontal component of lift, and it *is* the centripetal force, equal to mV²/R toward the centre. Adding an outward force too makes the net horizontal force zero, which would mean straight flight. A centrifugal term appears legitimately only when Newton's law is written in a frame rotating with the aircraft about the turn centre, as the −mω × (ω × ρ) term of (c), an "inertial" or fictitious force, and then the aircraft is at rest in that frame. *Proficient:* the definition and the order-of-magnitude terms with the judgement for each case; both acceleration decompositions with named terms and the radar/pilot distinction with the r̈ ≠ V̇ example; the transport theorem applied twice to five named terms with an example each; the frame argument against the outward force.

**2.** (a) The rotation group is three-dimensional but topologically not a flat space: it cannot be covered by a single chart of three coordinates without a point where the map from coordinates to attitude loses rank (a classical result; the "hairy ball" argument is the usual intuition). For 3-2-1 angles, φ̇ = p + (q sin φ + r cos φ) tan θ and ψ̇ = (q sin φ + r cos φ)/cos θ: both blow up at θ = ±90°, where the yaw and roll axes line up and only ψ − φ (or ψ + φ) is defined (question 11(c)). This is gimbal lock: in a three-gimbal platform the outer and inner gimbal axes become parallel and one rotational freedom is lost. Platforms add a fourth gimbal or avoid the attitude; flight software propagates a DCM or a quaternion and only converts to Euler angles for display, or switches to a different sequence near the singularity. (b) C is orthonormal (CCᵀ = I) with det C = +1; CCᵀ = I is six independent scalar constraints (it is symmetric), leaving 9 − 6 = 3 freedoms, and det = +1 picks the proper rotations. Numerical integration of Ċ = −[ω×]C is not exact, so C drifts off the set of rotations: its columns stop being unit length and orthogonal (question 11(d): 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ after 1000 s of RK4 at Δt = 0.5 s). Restore by re-orthonormalising: the nearest rotation from the SVD (C = UΣVᵀ → UVᵀ), or the cheaper first-order correction C ← C − ½(CCᵀ − I)C, applied every few steps. (c) A unit quaternion is q = (cos(φ/2), sin(φ/2) e) for a rotation by φ about the unit axis e, with q₀² + q₁² + q₂² + q₃² = 1. q and −q correspond to φ and φ + 2π about the same axis, which is the same attitude: the unit quaternions cover the rotations twice. A vector rotates as v′ = q ⊗ (0, v) ⊗ q* (or with q* first for the passive transformation of components, as in question 12). Kinematics: q̇ = ½ q ⊗ (0, ω) with ω in body axes. Reasons: no singularity anywhere; four numbers with one constraint, cheap to renormalise; products compose rotations cheaply; interpolation (SLERP) is natural; star trackers output quaternions directly. (d) I₁ω̇₁ + (I₃ − I₂)ω₂ω₃ = M₁ and cyclic, with ω and M in principal *body* axes; the equations are Ḣ_inertial = M written with the transport theorem in the rotating body frame. Two exact integrals of the torque-free motion, T = ½ΣI_iω_i² and H² = ΣI_i²ω_i², are ellipsoids in ω-space; ω must lie on their intersection (the polhode). Near the axes of largest and smallest I the intersections are small closed loops around the axis: stable. Near the intermediate axis the two ellipsoids touch along crossing curves through the axis (the separatrix): a small perturbation carries ω along it to the opposite side, the Dzhanibekov flip (question 13). With energy dissipation (flexible antennas, fuel slosh, a nutation damper) the angular momentum of the whole body is still conserved, because the dissipation is internal, but kinetic energy is not: it decreases, and T = H²/(2I) is lowest for rotation about the axis of *largest* I. So a minor-axis spinner drifts away from its spin axis toward a flat spin about the major axis. Explorer 1 (1958), long and thin and spun about its long axis, had flexible wire antennas that dissipated energy, and it ended up tumbling about a transverse axis; this became the major-axis rule for spin-stabilised spacecraft. *Proficient:* the three-number argument with the tan θ and 1/cos θ singularity and gimbal lock described; six constraints, drift and one restoration method; the half-angle definition, the double cover, the rotation formula and the kinematic equation with two reasons; Euler's equations in body axes, the two-ellipsoid stability argument, and the H-conserved, T-dissipated major-axis rule with Explorer 1.

**3.** (a) Generalised coordinates are any set of independent numbers that fix the configuration; the number of them is the number of degrees of freedom when the constraints are holonomic, that is, expressible as relations among the coordinates (and time). Double pendulum: two angles, 2 DOF (the inextensible links are holonomic constraints |r₁| = l₁, |r₂ − r₁| = l₂). Four-bar linkage: three moving links have 9 coordinates in the plane, the four pins impose 8 constraints, 1 DOF (the crank angle); the loop-closure equations are the holonomic constraints. A wheel rolling without slip on a straight line: x = rθ, 1 DOF; the rolling constraint ẋ = rθ̇ integrates to x − rθ = const, so it is holonomic. A disc rolling on a plane with a free heading: the no-slip condition relates velocities through the heading angle, and cannot be integrated to a relation among coordinates (the disc can reach any position with any orientation by rolling along suitable paths), so it is non-holonomic and needs Lagrange multipliers. (b) d/dt(∂L/∂q̇_i) − ∂L/∂q_i = Q_i, with L = T − V and Q_i the generalised forces of the non-conservative loads (Q_i = ΣF·∂r/∂q_i, from virtual work). Ideal constraint forces (string tensions, pin forces, rolling contact without slip) do no virtual work and never appear; for a slung load the two cable tensions vanish from the equations, leaving two coupled equations in two angles, where Newton–Euler would need four component equations plus two tension unknowns. What you lose is the constraint forces themselves: the tension the cable and hook must carry is needed for design, and must be recovered afterward (by Newton–Euler on each mass with the Lagrange accelerations, question 15(d)) or by Lagrange multipliers. (c) A coordinate that does not appear in L (only its rate does) is cyclic; its conjugate momentum ∂L/∂q̇ is conserved. In the Kepler problem in polar coordinates, θ is cyclic and p_θ = mr²θ̇, the angular momentum, is conserved (Kepler's second law). (d) ★ H(q, p) = Σp_iq̇_i − L with p_i = ∂L/∂q̇_i; Hamilton's equations q̇ = ∂H/∂p, ṗ = −∂H/∂q. H equals T + V when the kinetic energy is a homogeneous quadratic in the velocities and the constraints do not depend on time; it is conserved when H has no explicit time dependence. Liouville: the flow of Hamilton's equations preserves phase-space volume (area in one degree of freedom). An integrator whose step map does not preserve area (explicit Euler expands it by 1 + h²ω² per step; RK4 contracts it slightly) produces a systematic, secular drift in energy that grows without bound, whatever its local error; a symplectic integrator preserves area exactly and keeps the energy error bounded and oscillating for exponentially long times, which is what a long orbit propagation needs. *Proficient:* the definitions and the 2, 1, 1 counts with the integrability argument; Lagrange with Q_i, the missing constraint forces as both advantage and loss; cyclic coordinate θ and conserved angular momentum. Part (d) is graded only if attempted.

**4.** (a) For m ẍ + c ẋ + k x = F: ω_n = √(k/m); ζ = c/(2√(km)); ω_d = ω_n√(1 − ζ²); logarithmic decrement δ = ln(x_k/x_{k+1}) = 2πζ/√(1 − ζ²); Q ≈ 1/(2ζ) (the peak amplification at light damping, and f_n/Δf from the half-power bandwidth). A free-decay record gives ω_d (from the zero crossings or the peaks) and δ, hence ζ and ω_n; a frequency sweep gives the peak frequency, the peak amplification and the bandwidth, hence Q and ω_n. The displacement amplitude under a constant-amplitude force peaks at ω_n√(1 − 2ζ²), slightly below ω_n; the velocity amplitude peaks exactly at ω_n, and the phase of displacement behind force is exactly 90° at ω_n, which is how resonance is identified in modal testing. (b) A normal mode is a pattern of motion φ in which every coordinate moves harmonically at one frequency with fixed amplitude ratios: K φ = ω² M φ. For distinct frequencies, φ_iᵀMφ_j = 0 and φ_iᵀKφ_j = 0 for i ≠ j (from symmetry of M and K); with mass normalisation ΦᵀMΦ = I and ΦᵀKΦ = diag(ω²). The transformation x = Φη uncouples M ẍ + Kx = F into independent SDOF equations η̈_i + ω_i²η_i = φ_iᵀF. Damping stays uncoupled if ΦᵀCΦ is diagonal, which holds for proportional (Rayleigh) damping C = αM + βK, or if modal damping ratios are simply assumed. (c) A consistent-mass finite-element model with conforming elements is a Rayleigh–Ritz approximation: it restricts the displacement to a finite set of admissible shapes, which makes the structure artificially stiffer, so every frequency is an upper bound that converges from above (question 19(b)). For a design that must keep its first mode *above* a limit, a coarse model overstates the margin: the check must be made on a converged mesh (or with a lumped-mass model, which usually errs the other way, used as a bracket). (d) The one-sided PSD S(f) is the mean-square value per unit bandwidth, so ∫S df over all f is the mean square, and Grms is its square root (in g). "3σ" is meaningful as a design peak if the signal is stationary and Gaussian with zero mean, so that |x| exceeds 3σ 0.27% of the time. Miles: Grms = √((π/2) f_n Q S(f_n)) for a single-degree-of-freedom response to a base input whose PSD is flat near f_n, with light damping (Q ≳ 5), f_n in Hz. Isolation: the absolute transmissibility is below 1 only for f/f_n > √2; below that the mount amplifies, most at resonance. An isolator must therefore put its own natural frequency well below the band to be blocked, accepting amplification in the band around f_n. *Proficient:* the five definitions with what each record gives and the velocity/phase statement; the mode, orthogonality, uncoupling and Rayleigh damping; the upper-bound argument with its design consequence; PSD, Grms, Gaussian stationarity, Miles with its assumptions, and √2.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp


def quat_mul(a, b):
    """Hamilton product, scalar first."""
    a0, av = a[0], np.asarray(a[1:]); b0, bv = b[0], np.asarray(b[1:])
    return np.r_[a0 * b0 - av @ bv, a0 * bv + b0 * av + np.cross(av, bv)]


def quat_to_matrix(q):
    """Active rotation body -> inertial: v_I = R(q) v_B."""
    q0, q1, q2, q3 = np.asarray(q) / np.linalg.norm(q)
    return np.array([[1 - 2 * (q2**2 + q3**2), 2 * (q1 * q2 - q0 * q3), 2 * (q1 * q3 + q0 * q2)],
                     [2 * (q1 * q2 + q0 * q3), 1 - 2 * (q1**2 + q3**2), 2 * (q2 * q3 - q0 * q1)],
                     [2 * (q1 * q3 - q0 * q2), 2 * (q2 * q3 + q0 * q1), 1 - 2 * (q1**2 + q2**2)]])


def propagate_rigid_body(I, w0, q0, t_end, n_out=2001, rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12):
    """Torque-free rigid body: Euler's equations in principal body axes plus q_dot = 0.5 q (x) [0, w]."""
    I = np.asarray(I, float)

    def rhs(t, y):
        w, q = y[:3], y[3:]
        wd = -np.cross(w, I * w) / I
        return np.r_[wd, 0.5 * quat_mul(q, np.r_[0.0, w])]

    t = np.linspace(0.0, t_end, n_out)
    s = solve_ivp(rhs, (0.0, t_end), np.r_[w0, q0], t_eval=t, rtol=rtol, atol=atol, method="DOP853")
    q = s.y[3:].T
    return s.t, s.y[:3].T, q / np.linalg.norm(q, axis=1, keepdims=True)
```
