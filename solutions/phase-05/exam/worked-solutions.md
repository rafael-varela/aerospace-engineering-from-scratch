## Part A

**1.** (a) Forces on the leg alone: the tyre load at the axle (vertical and aft components, exerted by the runway through the wheel), the drag-brace force at C (exerted by the brace), the two components of the pin force at the top (exerted by the wing fitting), and the leg's own weight (exerted by the Earth; usually negligible against landing loads and dropped with a sentence). The brace is pinned at both ends and carries no load between them, so the only forces on it act at two points; for it to be in equilibrium those two forces must be equal, opposite and collinear, which forces them to lie along the line joining the pins. Its effect on the leg is therefore one unknown magnitude along a known line. The pin at the top transmits a force in any direction in the plane (no moment), so it is two unknowns. A member is a two-force member when it is loaded only at two points, with no moment applied at either and no load in between. A landing-gear leg itself is the classic counter-example: it is a long strut, but it carries the tyre load at the axle, the brace force at C and the pin force at O, three points, so it carries bending (question 5(c): 6.0 kN m at C). A strut with a distributed air load on it (a wing lift strut in flight) is another. (b) 2-D: ΣF_x = 0, ΣF_y = 0, ΣM_z = 0, three independent equations; 3-D: ΣF = 0 and ΣM = 0 as vectors, six. A planar pin-jointed truss with m members, r reaction components and j joints gives 2j equations in m + r unknowns: m + r < 2j is a mechanism, m + r = 2j may be determinate, m + r > 2j is indeterminate. The count is necessary but not sufficient, because the equations can be dependent: three rollers whose reactions are all parallel (all vertical) give r = 3 but cannot resist a horizontal load; three reactions whose lines of action meet at one point cannot resist a moment about it. The rank of the equilibrium matrix, not the count, is the test, which is what the lesson 02 and 03 solvers compute. (c) Besides equilibrium, an indeterminate problem needs compatibility (the deformations of the parts must fit together: all four wheels touch the floor) and a constitutive law (how much each part deflects per unit force: the tyre and leg stiffnesses). With four wheels on a slightly uneven floor, the wheel on a low spot carries less, and the load split depends on the floor's flatness relative to the tyres' deflection: a stiff trolley on a warped floor can stand on three wheels. (d) A free-body diagram shows the forces *on* the chosen body from everything outside its boundary. The brace's force on the leg is one force; the leg's equal and opposite force on the brace acts on the brace, which is outside the boundary, so it does not appear. Drawing both on the leg double-counts the interaction and the two cancel, which leaves the leg with no brace at all. *Proficient:* the four forces with their sources, the two-force argument (two points, no moments, collinear) and the leg as a counter-example; 3 and 6 equations, m + r = 2j with a parallel-reaction or concurrent-reaction counter-example and the rank test; compatibility and constitutive law named with the floor-flatness consequence; the third-law/boundary argument.

**2.** (a) Normal stress σ = lim ΔF_n/ΔA and shear stress τ = lim ΔF_t/ΔA, the force components normal and tangential to a small cut of area ΔA around the point, as ΔA → 0. The traction depends on the orientation of the cut, and the rule that gives the traction on any plane from its normal is linear: t = σn, a second-order tensor with nine components in 3-D. Moment equilibrium of a small cube about its own centre requires the shear stresses on adjacent faces to balance (τ_xy dy dz × dx = τ_yx dx dz × dy), so τ_xy = τ_yx and only six are independent. (b) Normal strain ε_x = ∂u/∂x (change of length per length) and engineering shear strain γ_xy = ∂u/∂y + ∂v/∂x (change of a right angle); these definitions are *compatibility*: kinematics, how strains follow from a continuous displacement field. Hooke's law is the constitutive law: ε_x = (σ_x − ν(σ_y + σ_z))/E, γ_xy = τ_xy/G, with G = E/(2(1 + ν)) for an isotropic material. Poisson's ratio is the ratio of the lateral contraction to the axial extension under uniaxial stress; the volumetric strain is ε_x + ε_y + ε_z = (1 − 2ν)(σ_x + σ_y + σ_z)/E, so ν = 0.5 makes it zero for any stress: incompressible (rubber is close). (c) Saint-Venant: the stress distribution produced by statically equivalent loads differs only within a distance of about the largest cross-section dimension from where they are applied. Away from the grips and from any hole, a bar's stress is P/A whatever the detail of the load application; next to a hole the cross-section changes abruptly and the stress concentrates, by a factor K_t = 3 at the edge of a small circular hole in a wide plate under uniaxial tension. Fatigue cracks start where the local stress range is largest; in a pressurised fuselage the hoop stress cycles once per flight and every rivet hole triples it locally (more with the rivet's own bearing load), so cracks start at the holes, which is why fuselage skins are inspected along rivet rows. (d) Linear elasticity assumes stress proportional to strain (the material is below its proportional limit) and small displacements (equilibrium can be written on the undeformed shape). Material failure: any stress above the proportional limit (the 0.2% offset yield is already past it); check the equivalent stress against F_ty. Geometric failure: large deflections, where equilibrium on the deformed shape matters; check δ/L, and the slope, against a few percent; in the cantilever lab at δ/L = 0.08 the elastica differs from the linear result by 0.7% (question 22). Buckling is the geometric failure in compression (question 3(d)). *Proficient:* the limit definitions with the tensor (nine components, six independent by moment equilibrium of a cube); strains as compatibility, Hooke's law with G = E/(2(1 + ν)) as constitutive, ν = 0.5 from the volumetric strain; Saint-Venant with K_t = 3 and the rivet-hole fatigue argument; one material and one geometric failure, each with its check.

**3.** (a) Limit load: the largest load expected in service (the most severe manoeuvre or gust the aircraft is certified for). Ultimate load: limit load × 1.5. The factor 1.0 to yield says the structure must carry limit load without detrimental permanent deformation (it must still work after the worst expected event); the 1.5 to ultimate says it must carry 1.5 × limit without failing, a reserve that covers the uncertainty in loads, material scatter beyond the statistical allowable, workmanship and analysis error. MS = allowable/(FS × applied) − 1; MS = +0.00 means the part exactly meets the requirement, and anything negative fails. A large positive margin on primary structure is reported because it is mass that need not be there, or a sign that the load or the failure mode was wrong (question 23, item 3: a margin of +3.00 on a strut that actually fails by buckling). (b) Tresca (maximum shear stress): yield when (σ_max − σ_min)/2 = σ_y/2, i.e. σ_max − σ_min = σ_y, from the idea that yielding is slip on the plane of maximum shear. Von Mises (distortion energy, or octahedral shear): yield when √(½((σ₁ − σ₂)² + (σ₂ − σ₃)² + (σ₃ − σ₁)²)) = σ_y, from the idea that the energy of shape change, not of volume change, causes yield (hydrostatic pressure does not). In pure shear τ, Tresca gives σ_eq = 2τ and von Mises √3τ: ratio 2/√3 = 1.155. Tresca is always at least as conservative, by at most 15.5% (in pure shear); they agree in uniaxial stress. (c) Brittle materials do not yield; they fracture, and their tensile strength is much lower than their compressive strength, which neither shear criterion represents. Mohr–Coulomb for σ₁ > 0 > σ₃: σ₁/S_t − σ₃/S_c = 1/n. In pure shear (σ₁ = τ, σ₃ = −τ) failure at τ = 1/(1/S_t + 1/S_c) = S_tS_c/(S_t + S_c) = 0.8S_t when S_c = 4S_t (question 17(d): 120 MPa for S_t = 150 MPa). (d) Buckling: the strut loses stability, a bifurcation at which a bent shape becomes an equilibrium. A stress criterion checks the material at a known deformed state; buckling is a loss of uniqueness of that state at a load set by stiffness (EI) and geometry (L), not by strength. Equilibrium is applied differently: every other analysis writes it on the undeformed geometry (small displacements), but buckling requires equilibrium of the *deflected* shape, where the axial load times the lateral deflection is a bending moment (EI w'' + Pw = 0). *Proficient:* limit and ultimate defined with what each factor protects and MS written; the large-margin finding; both criteria with their physical basis, 2/√3 and 15.5%; the brittle argument with Mohr–Coulomb and 0.8S_t; buckling as a stability failure with equilibrium on the deformed shape.

**4.** (a) Plane sections remain plane and normal to the deformed axis, so the axial strain varies linearly across the depth: ε = −y/ρ (curvature 1/ρ). Linear elasticity: σ = Eε = −Ey/ρ. Equilibrium: zero net axial force puts the neutral axis through the centroid (∫y dA = 0), and the moment of the stresses equals M: M = −∫σy dA = (E/ρ)I, so σ = −My/I. Small slopes make the curvature 1/ρ ≈ v'', so EI v'' = M. The load must act in a plane of symmetry (or through the shear centre, along a principal axis) for the bending to stay in that plane. Shear deformation adds a deflection of order PL/(GA) to the bending deflection PL³/(3EI); the ratio is of order (E/G)(h/L)², about 0.3 × 2.6 × (h/L)² for a solid rectangle. A wing spar with h/L ≈ 0.2/5 = 0.04 gives about 10⁻³; a deep fitting with h/L ≈ 0.5 gives about 0.2, so Euler–Bernoulli is good for spars and poor for short deep members. (b) A slice dx with a distributed load w (positive downward) and the sign convention of the preamble: vertical equilibrium V − w dx − (V + dV) = 0 gives dV/dx = −w; moments about the right face give M + V dx − w dx²/2 − (M + dM) = 0, so to first order dM/dx = V. Consequences: a point load makes a jump in V (equal to the load) and a kink in M; a concentrated couple makes a jump in M (equal to the couple) and no change in V; where V = 0, M has a stationary point (a local extremum), but not necessarily the global maximum, which can sit at a jump or at a support (question 12). (c) Castigliano's second theorem: for a linear-elastic structure, the deflection at a load P in its direction is ∂U/∂P, U being the strain energy expressed in terms of the loads. It requires linear elasticity (so U is the complementary energy too) and small displacements. A dummy load Q is added where the deflection is wanted, U is differentiated with respect to Q, and Q is then set to zero. Unit-load method: δ = ∫ M m/(EI) dx + Σ F f L/(AE) + …, where m and f are the internal forces caused by a unit load at the point; for a linear structure m = ∂M/∂Q, so the two integrals are identical term by term. (d) Maxwell–Betti: in a linear-elastic structure, the deflection at point A due to a load at B equals the deflection at B due to the same load at A (more generally, the work of load set 1 through the displacements of set 2 equals that of set 2 through set 1). Test on a wing: put dial gauges at two stations A and B; hang a mass at B and read the deflection at A; move the same mass to A and read B; the two readings should agree within the measurement uncertainty. A disagreement means non-linearity (slip in a joint, large deflection) or a misplaced load. *Proficient:* the four assumptions with the one-line derivations of σ = −My/I and EI v'' = M, the (h/L)² criterion with the spar/fitting contrast; the slice derivation and the three diagram consequences; Castigliano with its linearity requirement, the dummy load and m = ∂M/∂Q; reciprocity stated with a two-gauge test.

**21.** A reference implementation (any equivalent one passes):

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import eigh_tridiagonal


def buckling_load_fd(EI, L, n, ends="pinned-pinned"):
    """Lowest critical load of a uniform column from EI w'' + P w = 0, central differences on n intervals.

    pinned-pinned: w(0) = w(L) = 0, unknowns at the n - 1 interior nodes.
    fixed-free: v = delta - w (deflection measured from the tip) obeys EI v'' + P v = 0 with v'(0) = 0 at
    the clamp (ghost node v_-1 = v_1) and v(L) = 0; unknowns at nodes 0 .. n-1.
    Returns (P_cr, x, mode) with max |mode| = 1.
    """
    h = L / n
    if ends == "pinned-pinned":
        m = n - 1
        d, e = np.full(m, 2.0) / h**2, np.full(m - 1, -1.0) / h**2
        lam, vec = eigh_tridiagonal(d, e, select="i", select_range=(0, 0))
        x = np.linspace(h, L - h, m)
    elif ends == "fixed-free":
        m = n
        d, e = np.full(m, 2.0) / h**2, np.full(m - 1, -1.0) / h**2
        e[0] = -np.sqrt(2.0) / h**2          # ghost row (2, -2) symmetrised by scaling node 0 by sqrt(2)
        lam, vec = eigh_tridiagonal(d, e, select="i", select_range=(0, 0))
        x = np.linspace(0.0, L - h, m)
        vec = vec.copy()
        vec[0] /= np.sqrt(2.0)                # undo the scaling
    else:
        raise ValueError(ends)
    mode = vec[:, 0] / np.max(np.abs(vec[:, 0]))
    return EI * lam[0], x, mode


def euler_load(EI, L, K):
    return np.pi**2 * EI / (K * L) ** 2
```

import numpy as np
import pytest

EI, L = 205e9 * 5.1477e-9, 0.80


def test_pinned_pinned_matches_euler():
    p, x, mode = buckling_load_fd(EI, L, 200)
    assert p == pytest.approx(euler_load(EI, L, 1.0), rel=1e-4)
    assert np.allclose(np.abs(mode), np.sin(np.pi * x / L), atol=1e-3)


def test_second_order_convergence():
    exact = euler_load(EI, L, 1.0)
    e1 = abs(buckling_load_fd(EI, L, 50)[0] - exact)
    e2 = abs(buckling_load_fd(EI, L, 100)[0] - exact)
    assert e1 / e2 == pytest.approx(4.0, abs=0.1)
    assert buckling_load_fd(EI, L, 50)[0] < exact


def test_fixed_free_effective_length_two():
    p, _, _ = buckling_load_fd(EI, L, 200, "fixed-free")
    assert p == pytest.approx(euler_load(EI, L, 2.0), rel=1e-4)
    assert p == pytest.approx(buckling_load_fd(EI, 2 * L, 400)[0], rel=1e-4)
