## Part A

**1.** (a) cos(10⁻⁴) = 0.999 999 995 000 000 04…, and the subtraction 1 − cos θ = 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ is exact; the damage is done before it, when cos θ is rounded to the nearest double: its absolute error is up to ε/2 ≈ 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁶, and that absolute error is inherited unchanged by a result of size 5 × 10⁻⁹, so the relative error is about 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁶/5 × 10⁻⁹ ≈ 2 × 10⁻⁸ (observed: 5.2 × 10⁻⁹, within the bound). Of the 16 significant figures of double precision about 8 survive: the eight leading zeros of the true result were "stored" as the digits of cos θ that cancelled. (b) In single precision ε/2 = 6 × 10⁻⁸ > 5 × 10⁻⁹, so cos θ rounds to exactly 1.0f and 1 − cos θ returns exactly 0: a 100% relative error. (c) Catastrophic cancellation: subtracting two nearly equal numbers removes the leading digits and promotes the rounding error of the operands to the leading digit of the result. Use 1 − cos θ = 2 sin²(θ/2): sin(θ/2) ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁵ is computed with relative error ε, squaring and doubling keep a relative error of order ε, and no subtraction of close numbers occurs (single precision then returns 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ correctly). The same cure fixes the small root of a quadratic (use the product of the roots) and the difference of nearby logarithms (`log1p`). (d) κ = θ sin θ/(1 − cos θ) → θ·θ/(θ²/2) = 2 as θ → 0 (evaluated: 2.000): a relative perturbation of θ changes f by only twice that, so the *problem* is well conditioned. Conditioning is a property of the mathematical map (how much the exact answer moves when the input moves); stability is a property of the algorithm (how much extra error the finite-precision steps introduce beyond what conditioning forces). Here the problem is well conditioned and the direct algorithm is unstable, which is exactly the case a rewrite can fix; an ill-conditioned problem no algorithm can rescue. *Proficient:* the ε/result argument with about 8 figures lost, the single-precision zero, cancellation named and the 2 sin² rewrite with the reason, κ ≈ 2 and the conditioning-versus-stability distinction applied correctly.

**2.** (a) The local truncation error is the error of one step started from the exact solution, O(h^{p+1}) for a method of order p; the global error at a fixed final time is the accumulated error over the N = T/h steps, O(h^p): one power of h is lost because the number of steps grows as 1/h and errors propagate (amplified by at most e^{LT} for a Lipschitz constant L). Convergence = consistency (local error order at least 1) + zero-stability (the method applied to y′ = 0 does not amplify perturbations); that is the Dahlquist equivalence theorem, and for one-step methods zero-stability is automatic. (b) Applied to y′ = λy with z = hλ, every Runge–Kutta method gives y_{n+1} = R(z) y_n with R a polynomial (explicit) or rational function (implicit) approximating e^z. The stability region is {z : |R(z)| ≤ 1}; inside it the computed mode decays or is bounded as the true mode does for Re λ ≤ 0; outside it |R| > 1 and the numerical solution grows exponentially even though the exact one decays: a stability error that no amount of step-halving within the unstable range reduces, until the step crosses into the region. (c) A system is stiff when it has eigenvalues whose magnitudes differ by orders of magnitude and the fast modes have died out over the time span of interest, so that the solution is smooth but the step of an explicit method is still limited by the *fastest* eigenvalue through the stability region: the step is set by stability, not by accuracy. RK45 does not blow up: its error estimate detects the incipient instability as a large error and shrinks the step to the stability limit, so it takes roughly T|λ_max|/3 tiny steps across a smooth solution. (d) h_new = h (tol/err)^{1/(p+1)} (with a safety factor and limits on the ratio). The exponent is 1/(p + 1) because the quantity being controlled is the *local* error of the step, which scales as h^{p+1}; using 1/p would control the global-error rate and would under-correct. *Proficient:* local versus global with the lost power of h and the theorem named, R(z) and what leaving the region does, stiffness with "stability not accuracy", and the update with the p + 1 reason.

**3.** (a) Leapfrog is symplectic: its one-step map preserves the symplectic two-form, which in one degree of freedom means it preserves area in the (x, v) phase plane exactly, as the true Hamiltonian flow does (Liouville's theorem). RK4 is not symplectic; its map contracts phase-space area by a factor slightly less than one per step on an oscillatory mode (|R(ih ω)|² − 1 ≈ −(hω)⁶/72 per step for the harmonic oscillator), so the energy, a function of the phase-space radius, decays by a fixed fraction per step and the loss accumulates linearly in time. (b) A symplectic integrator exactly conserves a *modified* (shadow) Hamiltonian H̃ = H + h²H₂ + h⁴H₄ + …, a nearby function of the state; the true energy H then differs from the conserved H̃ by O(h²) terms that are periodic along the orbit, so the energy error oscillates with bounded amplitude and never drifts (for exponentially long times). It is not zero because H̃ ≠ H. (c) For a long fixed-step propagation the quantity that matters is the secular error: RK4's energy drift and the phase drift that goes with it grow without bound, so after enough orbits a fourth-order method with linearly growing drift is worse than a second-order method with bounded error; leapfrog's energy error at the same step is larger at first but stays put. RK4 (or better, an adaptive high-order method) is the right tool for short propagations where the local accuracy per step matters, for non-Hamiltonian systems (drag, thrust, where there is no energy to conserve) and for problems where variable step size is essential, because symplecticity is lost under step-size changes unless done carefully. (d) Time-reversible means that integrating forward n steps and then integrating backward n steps (negate h, or negate v and integrate forward) returns exactly to the starting state, up to round-off. Test: propagate an orbit N steps, flip the velocity sign, propagate N more steps, assert the position matches the initial position to round-off (10⁻¹² relative), and assert that RK4 fails the same test by an amount that scales as h⁴. *Proficient:* symplectic/area preservation versus RK4 contraction, the shadow Hamiltonian explanation of bounded-not-zero, secular versus local error with when RK4 is right, and the reversibility test.

**4.** (a) CG requires A symmetric positive definite. The five-point Laplacian with Dirichlet boundaries is symmetric by construction (the stencil is the same in both directions) and its eigenvalues are 4[sin²(iπ/(2(N+1))) + sin²(jπ/(2(N+1)))] for i, j = 1…N, all positive, so it is positive definite (equivalently it is the Gram-like matrix of a discrete energy ½Σ(u_{i+1} − u_i)² + … that vanishes only for u = 0 with zero boundaries). (b) The residuals are mutually orthogonal, r_iᵀr_j = 0 for i ≠ j, and the search directions are A-conjugate, p_iᵀAp_j = 0 for i ≠ j; the k-th iterate minimises the A-norm of the error over x₀ + the Krylov space span{r₀, Ar₀, …, A^{k−1}r₀}. Since at most n orthogonal residuals fit in ℝⁿ, the n-th residual is zero: exact termination in at most n steps (fewer if A has fewer distinct eigenvalues "seen" by r₀). Nobody relies on it because rounding destroys the orthogonality after tens of iterations, because n steps of a sparse matrix-vector product is as expensive as a dense factorisation, and because the point of CG is to stop after k ≪ n iterations when the error bound is met. (c) ‖e_k‖_A ≤ 2((√κ − 1)/(√κ + 1))^k ‖e₀‖_A. With κ ≈ 4(N + 1)²/π², √κ ≈ 2(N + 1)/π ∝ N, so the number of iterations for a fixed tolerance grows linearly with N (the number of points per side, i.e. as 1/h), while each iteration costs one matrix-vector product with 5 non-zeros per row, O(N²) per iteration: total O(N³) for N² unknowns. For N = 100: κ = 4134, √κ = 64.3, ratio 0.9694, and 2 × 0.9694^k ≤ 10⁻⁶ needs k ≥ ln(2 × 10⁶)/0.03111 = 466 iterations. (An actual `scipy.sparse.linalg.cg` run on this matrix with b = 1 converged to 10⁻⁶ in 159 iterations, and in 320 for N = 200: the bound is pessimistic by a factor of three but the linear scaling with N is real.) (d) The diagonal of the five-point Laplacian is the constant 4, so Jacobi preconditioning is a scalar multiple of the identity and changes nothing in the Krylov space. What helps is a preconditioner that captures the long-range coupling: incomplete Cholesky, or a multigrid V-cycle, which makes the iteration count independent of N. *Proficient:* SPD with a reason, the two orthogonalities and finite termination with why it is not relied upon, the √κ bound with iterations ∝ N and cost ∝ N² per iteration giving 466 for N = 100, and the constant-diagonal argument against Jacobi with a working alternative named.

**10.** (a) With a_n = −ω²x_n: x_{n+1} = (1 − h²ω²/2)x_n + hv_n, and v_{n+1} = v_n + (h/2)(−ω²x_n − ω²x_{n+1}) = v_n − (hω²/2)[x_n + (1 − h²ω²/2)x_n + hv_n] = −hω²(1 − h²ω²/4)x_n + (1 − h²ω²/2)v_n. So

M = [[1 − h²ω²/2, h], [−hω²(1 − h²ω²/4), 1 − h²ω²/2]].

(b) det M = (1 − h²ω²/2)² + h²ω²(1 − h²ω²/4) = 1 − h²ω² + h⁴ω⁴/4 + h²ω² − h⁴ω⁴/4 = 1 (SymPy confirms); tr M = 2 − h²ω². det M = 1 means the linear map preserves area in the (x, v) plane: a small patch of initial conditions is sheared and rotated but keeps its area after every step. The exact flow of ẍ = −ω²x is a rotation in (x, v/ω) and also preserves area (Liouville's theorem; det e^{At} = e^{t·tr A} = 1 since tr A = 0), so the discrete map inherits the geometric property of the continuous one: that is what makes leapfrog symplectic.
(c) The eigenvalues satisfy μ² − (tr M)μ + 1 = 0, product 1. import numpy as np
```python
import pytest
from scipy.sparse.linalg import cg as scipy_cg


def conjugate_gradient(A, b: np.ndarray, tol: float = 1e-10, maxiter: int | None = None):
    """Solve A x = b for symmetric positive definite A by the conjugate gradient method.

    `A` may be a dense array or anything with a matrix-vector product via `A @ v`.
    Returns (x, history) where history is the list of residual 2-norms after each step.
    """
    n = b.size
    maxiter = n if maxiter is None else maxiter
    x = np.zeros(n)
    r = b - A @ x
    p = r.copy()
    rr = r @ r
    history = [np.sqrt(rr)]
    for _ in range(maxiter):
        if history[-1] <= tol * history[0]:
            break
        Ap = A @ p
        alpha = rr / (p @ Ap)
        x = x + alpha * p
        r = r - alpha * Ap
        rr_new = r @ r
        history.append(np.sqrt(rr_new))
        p = r + (rr_new / rr) * p
        rr = rr_new
    return x, history


def poisson_1d(n: int) -> np.ndarray:
    """n x n matrix of -u'' on (0, 1) with u(0) = u(1) = 0, grid spacing h = 1/(n + 1), scaled by h^2."""
    return np.diag(2.0 * np.ones(n)) - np.diag(np.ones(n - 1), 1) - np.diag(np.ones(n - 1), -1)


def test_poisson_matches_closed_form():
    n = 49
    h = 1.0 / (n + 1)
    x = np.linspace(h, 1 - h, n)
    A = poisson_1d(n)
    b = h * h * np.ones(n)                 # -u'' = 1  ->  u = x(1 - x)/2, exact at the nodes
    u, _ = conjugate_gradient(A, b, tol=1e-12)
    assert u == pytest.approx(0.5 * x * (1 - x), abs=1e-10)


def test_finite_termination_and_scipy_agreement():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 30
    M = rng.standard_normal((n, n))
    A = M @ M.T + n * np.eye(n)            # SPD by construction
    b = rng.standard_normal(n)
    x, hist = conjugate_gradient(A, b, tol=1e-12)
    assert len(hist) - 1 <= n              # at most n steps
    assert x == pytest.approx(np.linalg.solve(A, b), rel=1e-8)
    x_scipy, info = scipy_cg(A, b, rtol=1e-12, atol=0.0)
    assert info == 0 and x == pytest.approx(x_scipy, rel=1e-7)


def test_error_decreases_monotonically_in_the_A_norm():
    n = 40
    A = poisson_1d(n)
    x_exact = np.sin(np.pi * np.arange(1, n + 1) / (n + 1))
    b = A @ x_exact
    errs = []
    for k in range(1, 12):                 # re-run with maxiter = k to sample the k-th iterate
        x, _ = conjugate_gradient(A, b, tol=0.0, maxiter=k)
        e = x - x_exact
        errs.append(np.sqrt(e @ A @ e))
    assert all(errs[i + 1] < errs[i] for i in range(len(errs) - 1))
```

All three tests pass (`pytest -q`: 3 passed). Two observations from running them: the 1-D Poisson system with n = 49 and a constant right-hand side converges to 10⁻¹⁵ in 25 iterations, not 49, because the constant vector has components only on the odd eigenmodes, so CG "sees" only 25 distinct eigenvalues and terminates there; and the residual 2-norm history of that run is *not* monotone (it rises on several steps), while the A-norm of the error is. That is why the third test uses the A-norm: CG is defined as the method that minimises ‖e‖_A over the growing Krylov space, so monotone decrease of ‖e‖_A is the property the algorithm guarantees and a bug would break, whereas the residual norm ‖r‖₂ = ‖e‖_{A²} is not minimised and can legitimately increase, so a test on it would fail for a correct implementation (a fourth useful test asserts that successive residuals are mutually orthogonal to 10⁻⁹, which they are). `scipy.sparse.linalg.cg` with a preconditioner (`M=`) is still the production call because the preconditioner is where all the performance is for a real Poisson or FEM matrix (iterations independent of the grid size with multigrid, against ∝ N without), because it works on any sparse format or `LinearOperator` without forming the matrix, and because its stopping criterion, breakdown handling and callbacks have been exercised on far more matrices than one exam. *Proficient:* a correct CG loop (one matrix-vector product per iteration, the α and β formulas right, residual updated recursively), the three tests present and passing, the A-norm-versus-residual explanation, and the preconditioner reason for the library call.
