# Phase 06 Exam: Dynamics & Vibrations

Integrated assessment of the whole phase. Suggested self-assessment mode: closed reference, a time limit chosen and recorded in advance (the exam has not been piloted, so no duration is suggested), no code except in Part C, no computer algebra in Part B; a scientific calculator (or Python used only as a calculator) is allowed for the arithmetic in Part B, since several questions need a 2 × 2 or 3 × 3 matrix product, a logarithm, an arctangent, a few square roots and one root of a quadratic by hand. There is no enforced lock; the point is to find out what you can do unaided. Solutions and pass thresholds are in `solutions/phase-06/exam/`.

Report your result per competency: (1) mathematical and physical reasoning, (2) analytical problem solving, (3) computational competence, (4) engineering interpretation. Questions are tagged with the competencies they assess. Every question is written over an aircraft or a spacecraft; the dynamics is what is being tested, and the framing is there so that you also practise deciding which model applies and what it assumes. The organising idea of the phase is *kinematics, kinetics, and an independent check*: in every question state the frame you work in (inertial or rotating, and which axes), write the kinematics before the forces, say what the model assumes and where it fails, and check the main result by an independent route (a closed form against a numerical integration, a conservation law, a second method, a limiting case). SI units throughout; angles in radians unless a degree sign is written. Use g = 9.81 m/s², Earth's rotation rate Ω = 7.292 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s, μ_Earth = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s², R_Earth = 6371 km and ρ_air = 1.2 kg/m³ unless a question gives another value. Attitude conventions: 3-2-1 (yaw ψ, pitch θ, roll φ) Euler angles from north–east–down (NED) axes to body axes; the direction cosine matrix C maps NED components to body components, v_B = C v_N; quaternions are written scalar first, q = (q₀, q₁, q₂, q₃), with the Hamilton product. Every data set in this exam is invented for the exam and is labelled synthetic; nothing here is a measurement, and no table was produced by a kit you have built. Where a question quotes the output of a simulation, it is a computed result, not a measurement. Part D question 24 is the exception in the other direction: if you have done the `p06-pendulum-sysid` experiment, answer it with *your own* data and say so; the synthetic table is for learners without the kit.

Question 16 is marked ★ and is optional, like lesson 12. Attempt it if you studied lesson 12; it can replace one other Part B question in the count (see the pass thresholds).

## Part A: Concepts

**1.** (1, 4) Frames and kinematics. (a) Say what an inertial frame is, why Newton's second law in the form F = ma holds only in one, and why a frame fixed to the runway is "inertial enough" for an arrested landing but not for a 48 km artillery trajectory or a Foucault pendulum. Give the order of magnitude of each term that the Earth's rotation adds at 45° N for a body moving at 250 m/s. (b) Write the velocity and acceleration of a particle in polar coordinates and in path (normal–tangential) coordinates, name each term, and say which description an air-traffic radar measures and which one a pilot feels. Say why the tangential acceleration in path coordinates is not the radial acceleration r̈ in polar coordinates. (c) State the transport theorem for the time derivative of a vector in a rotating frame and use it twice to derive the five-term acceleration of a point measured in a frame that both translates and rotates. Name each term and give one aerospace situation in which each matters. (d) A learner writes the centripetal acceleration of an aircraft in a level turn as an outward "centrifugal force" on the free-body diagram drawn in a runway-fixed frame. Say what is wrong, and say in which frame a centrifugal term legitimately appears and what it is then called.

**2.** (1) Rigid bodies in three dimensions. (a) Say why three numbers always suffice to describe an attitude but no set of three numbers can describe every attitude without a singularity. Show where the 3-2-1 Euler-angle kinematic equations become singular, what physical configuration of the gimbals this is, and what an inertial platform or a flight-control law does about it. (b) State the properties of a direction cosine matrix (orthonormality, determinant), say how many independent constraints those impose on its nine entries, and say what happens to them when the kinematic equation Ċ = −[ω×]C is integrated numerically. Give one way to restore them. (c) Say what a unit quaternion is, why q and −q represent the same attitude, how a vector is rotated with quaternion products, and what the quaternion kinematic equation is. Give two reasons why star trackers and flight software use quaternions. (d) Write Euler's equations for a rigid body in principal axes, say in which frame each vector is resolved, and explain without solving them why spin about the axes of largest and smallest moment of inertia is stable for a rigid body and spin about the intermediate axis is not. Then say why a real spacecraft spinning about its axis of *smallest* moment of inertia is not stable, which conserved quantity survives energy dissipation and which does not, and what Explorer 1 did.

**3.** (1) Analytical mechanics. (a) Define generalised coordinates, degrees of freedom and holonomic constraints for a double pendulum, a four-bar linkage and a wheel rolling without slipping on a straight runway. Say how many degrees of freedom each has and why the rolling constraint of the third is integrable (holonomic) while that of a disc rolling on a plane is not. (b) State Lagrange's equations with generalised forces, say what the Lagrangian is, and say which forces never appear in them and why that is the method's main practical advantage over Newton–Euler for a slung load. Say what you lose: which quantity Newton–Euler gives you that Lagrange does not, unless you add it back, and why a cable designer needs it. (c) Say what a cyclic (ignorable) coordinate is and what is conserved when one exists; give the cyclic coordinate in the Kepler problem and the quantity it conserves. (d) ★ (optional, lesson 12) Define the Hamiltonian and Hamilton's equations, say when the Hamiltonian equals the total energy, state Liouville's theorem, and explain why an integrator that does not preserve phase-space area cannot be trusted for long orbit propagation even if its local error is small.

**4.** (1, 4) Vibration. (a) Define natural frequency, damping ratio, damped natural frequency, logarithmic decrement and quality factor for a single-degree-of-freedom system, and say which of them you can read from a free-decay record and which from a frequency sweep. Say why "resonance at the natural frequency" is only approximately true for the displacement of a damped system and exactly true for what. (b) Say what a normal mode of a multi-degree-of-freedom system is, state the orthogonality relations of the modes with respect to the mass and stiffness matrices, and say what modal superposition does for the equations of motion. Say what assumption about damping keeps the modes uncoupled and name the most common one. (c) A continuous beam has infinitely many modes. Say why a finite-element model of it gives frequencies that are always *higher* than the exact ones when it uses a consistent mass matrix, and what that means for a design that must keep its first mode above a limit. (d) Define a power spectral density and Grms, say what assumption about the signal makes "3σ" a meaningful peak, state Miles' equation and its assumptions, and say why a vibration isolator must have its natural frequency well *below* the frequencies it is meant to block, giving the frequency ratio above which isolation begins.
