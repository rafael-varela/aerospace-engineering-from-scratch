# Phase 04 Exam: Physics Foundations

Integrated assessment of the whole phase. Suggested self-assessment mode: closed reference, a time limit chosen and recorded in advance (the exam has not been piloted, so no duration is suggested), no code except in Part C, no computer algebra in Part B; a scientific calculator (or Python used only as a calculator) is allowed for the arithmetic in Part B, since several questions need square roots, logarithms, an inverse hyperbolic cosine and a few decibels by hand. There is no enforced lock; the point is to find out what you can do unaided. Solutions and pass thresholds are in `solutions/phase-04/exam/`.

Report your result per competency: (1) mathematical and physical reasoning, (2) analytical problem solving, (3) computational competence, (4) engineering interpretation. Questions are tagged with the competencies they assess. Every question is written over an aerospace situation; the physics is what is being tested, the framing is there so that you also practise deciding which model applies and what it assumes. The organising idea of the phase is *conservation and modelling*: in every question name the conservation law or field equation that governs the system, say what the model assumes, and say where it fails. Angles are in radians unless a degree sign is written. SI units throughout. Use g = 9.81 m/s², ρ_air = 1.225 kg/m³, μ_air = 1.81 × 10⁻⁵ Pa s, R = 8.314 J/(mol K), k_B = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K, N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, μ_Earth = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s², R_Earth = 6371 km, c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s, μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m, Z₀ = 376.7 Ω, e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, m_p = 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, unless a question gives another value. Every data set in this exam is invented for the exam and is labelled synthetic; nothing here is a measurement, and no table was produced by a kit you have built. Part D question 20 is the exception in the other direction: if you have done the `p04-pendulum` or `p04-drag-drop` experiment, answer it with *your own* data and say so; the synthetic table is for learners without the kit.

## Part A: Concepts

**1.** (1, 4) A bean bag is dropped from 2 m and a paper cone is dropped beside it. (a) Draw the free-body diagram of each at an instant during the fall, name every force with the body that exerts it, and state Newton's second law for each as a differential equation. Say which Newton's-law statement forbids a "force of motion" on the diagram and why a diagram that includes one is wrong. (b) Say what *terminal speed* is in terms of the diagram, derive its expression for quadratic drag F_D = ½ρv²C_DA, and say what dimensionless number decides whether drag is quadratic or linear in v for an object of this size and speed. Which is it for a 4 cm ball at a few metres per second? (c) The cone reaches terminal speed; the bean bag does not. Say what single number from the model tells you in advance which of the two will show a measurable drag effect over 2 m in a 30 fps video, and how you would compute it. (d) The point-particle model treats both objects as a mass at one point. Name two things it cannot predict about the cone that you would see in the video, and say which assumption fails for each.

**2.** (1) A satellite is in an elliptical orbit about a spherical Earth. (a) Name the two quantities conserved along the orbit, state the condition on the force that makes each one conserved (write the time derivative of each and show what kills it), and say which of Kepler's laws is the geometric statement of angular-momentum conservation. (b) Write the specific orbital energy ε in terms of v and r and in terms of the semi-major axis a (the vis-viva relation); say what the sign of ε tells you and what ε = 0 means. (c) Atmospheric drag removes energy from a low orbit. Explain, from ε(a) and v(a), why a satellite that *loses* energy to drag *speeds up*, and say what quantity drag does to the orbit's shape over many revolutions and why. (d) In Phase 03 you propagated an orbit with leapfrog and with RK4 at the same step. Say which of the two conserved quantities each integrator preserves exactly (to round-off), which only approximately, and why that matters for deciding whether a slow energy drift in a simulation is physics (drag) or numerics.

**3.** (1, 4) Kinetic theory and the atmosphere. (a) Explain, in words and with one equation, where gas pressure comes from in the molecular model and why pressure is proportional to the mean of v², not of v. State what "temperature" measures in this model and what the equipartition theorem says about it. (b) Say why the speed of sound in air is of the same order as the molecular rms speed but not equal to it, and give the ratio in terms of γ. (c) Say what assumptions the ideal-gas model makes about molecules, name the condition under which each fails, and say which one fails first at 100 km altitude (give the dimensionless number that decides) and which one fails first in a high-pressure gas bottle. (d) The International Standard Atmosphere combines the ideal-gas law, hydrostatic equilibrium and a prescribed temperature profile. Say which of these three is physics and which is a convention, and what that means for a pilot reading a pressure altimeter on a hot day.

**4.** (1) Fields and circuits. (a) State Kirchhoff's current law and voltage law and say which conservation law each one expresses; say what assumption about the circuit (in terms of frequency and size) the voltage law requires, and which Maxwell equation it violates when that assumption fails. (b) State what Faraday's law and the Ampère–Maxwell law say, in words, about how changing fields produce each other, and say why their combination yields a wave with speed c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀). (c) A transmitter radiates P watts isotropically. Derive the inverse-square intensity law from energy conservation and the Poynting vector, and say why a real antenna's intensity has the same 1/r² dependence but a direction-dependent coefficient; say what a half-wave dipole's pattern looks like and where its nulls are. (d) A voltmeter and an ammeter each disturb the circuit they measure. Say how each is built (its internal resistance, large or small, and why), what "loading" means, and how you decide, before measuring, whether the loading error matters.
