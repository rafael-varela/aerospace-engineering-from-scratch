# Phase 08 Exam: Fluid Mechanics

Integrated assessment of the whole phase. Suggested self-assessment mode: closed reference, a time limit chosen and recorded in advance (the exam has not been piloted, so no duration is suggested), no code except in Part C, no computer algebra in Part B; a scientific calculator (or Python used only as a calculator) is allowed for the arithmetic in Part B, since several questions need logarithms, fractional powers, a few iterations of a root finder by hand and one numerical quadrature that you may do with Simpson's rule on five points. There is no enforced lock; the point is to find out what you can do unaided. Solutions and pass thresholds are in `solutions/phase-08/exam/`.

Report your result per competency: (1) mathematical and physical reasoning, (2) analytical problem solving, (3) computational competence, (4) engineering interpretation. Questions are tagged with the competencies they assess. Every question is written over an aircraft, a rocket, a wind tunnel or the home lab; the fluid mechanics is what is being tested, and the framing is there so that you also practise deciding which model applies and what it assumes. The organising idea of the phase is *conservation of mass, momentum and energy, applied to a control volume or to a fluid particle, with a model for the stress*. In every question say which conservation law each step uses, in which form (integral or differential), and what it assumes about the stress (hydrostatic, inviscid, Newtonian laminar, or a turbulence model); say where the model fails; and check the main result by an independent route (a second method, a limiting case, a balance that must close, or a published anchor value). SI units throughout; knots and feet do not appear.

## Part A: Concepts

**1.** (1, 4) The continuum, the conservation laws and the stress. (a) Define a fluid by its response to shear stress, and say what the continuum hypothesis assumes. Give the Knudsen number, estimate it for the light aeroplane's wing at 3000 m (mean free path of air there about 9 × 10⁻⁸ m) and for a sounding rocket at 100 km (mean free path about 0.1 m, body diameter 0.1 m), and say which of the two this phase's equations describe. (b) State the Reynolds transport theorem in words and in symbols for a fixed control volume, and say why it is the bridge between the laws of mechanics (written for a fixed mass) and the control-volume balances of lesson 03. State the differential continuity equation and what it reduces to for incompressible flow. (c) Name the four models for the stress that the phase uses (hydrostatic, inviscid, Newtonian laminar, turbulence model). For each, give the stress tensor (or what replaces it) in one line, one lesson that uses it, and one situation in which it fails. (d) Distinguish the Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions, and define the material derivative D/Dt = ∂/∂t + u·∇. Define streamline, pathline and streakline, say which one a smoke wire in a wind tunnel shows and which one a GPS-tracked balloon traces, and say when all three coincide.

**2.** (1) Inviscid flow, circulation and lift. (a) Derive Bernoulli's equation from Euler's equation along a streamline and list every assumption. State the stronger form that holds between *any* two points of an irrotational flow, and say what extra condition it needs. Name three places in this phase where Bernoulli fails and say which assumption fails in each (a fan, a pipe with friction, a pitot tube at Mach 0.8, a separated wake). (b) Define vorticity and circulation and state Stokes's theorem connecting them. State Kelvin's circulation theorem with its three conditions, and use it to explain why a wing starting from rest leaves behind a starting vortex whose circulation is equal and opposite to the bound circulation. (c) State the Kutta–Joukowski theorem and d'Alembert's paradox. Say how both can be true at once in two-dimensional potential flow, and what real flows have that potential flow lacks, so that bluff bodies have drag. Say what the Kutta condition supplies that potential flow by itself cannot. (d) A learner says "vorticity is created by the fluid rotating, so a fluid particle going round a free vortex v_θ = Γ/(2πr) must have vorticity". Say what is wrong, computing the vorticity of the free vortex away from its axis, and say where the vorticity of a real trailing vortex sits and how it got there.

**3.** (1) Boundary layers, separation and transition. (a) Say why a boundary layer forms at high Reynolds number, how its thickness scales with x and Re_x, and which terms of the Navier–Stokes equations Prandtl's boundary-layer equations keep and drop (and why the pressure is impressed by the outer flow). Define δ₉₉, δ* and θ and give a physical meaning for δ* that a wind-tunnel designer uses. (b) Explain separation: what an adverse pressure gradient does to the near-wall fluid, what the wall shear is at separation, why a laminar layer separates sooner than a turbulent one, and why Thwaites's method can predict laminar separation from the outer velocity alone. (c) Describe the route from a laminar to a turbulent boundary layer in a quiet free stream: linear instability (Tollmien–Schlichting waves), amplification, breakdown. Say what the Orr–Sommerfeld equation is (which assumptions reduce Navier–Stokes to it) and what its critical Reynolds number means and does *not* mean. State the idea of the e^N method and what N = 9 stands for. (d) Name two routes to transition that the e^N method of lesson 12 does not represent and say what each does to the transition location on the light aeroplane's wing.

**4.** (1, 4) Turbulence, its modelling, similarity and compressibility. (a) Write the Reynolds decomposition and derive, for the x-momentum equation of an incompressible flow, where the Reynolds stresses come from. Count unknowns and equations for the Reynolds-averaged equations in 3-D and state the closure problem. State the Boussinesq eddy-viscosity hypothesis and say what physical behaviour it cannot represent. (b) State Kolmogorov's hypotheses and give the Kolmogorov length, time and velocity scales in terms of ν and ε; give the −5/3 law and say over what range of wavenumbers it applies. Say what Taylor's frozen-turbulence hypothesis assumes and when it fails. (c) State the Buckingham Pi theorem, say why the number of groups is n − rank of the dimension matrix (not n − number of dimensions), and say why a 1:5 wind-tunnel model of the light aeroplane cannot match both the Reynolds and the Mach number of flight in an atmospheric tunnel. Say which one matters more for the drag of the aeroplane at cruise and why. (d) State the rule of thumb that flow is "incompressible below Mach 0.3", say what quantity it bounds and by how much, and say why it is a statement about the flow and not about the gas.
