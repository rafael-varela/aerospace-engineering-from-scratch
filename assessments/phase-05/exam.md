# Phase 05 Exam: Statics & Mechanics of Materials

Integrated assessment of the whole phase. Suggested self-assessment mode: closed reference, a time limit chosen and recorded in advance (the exam has not been piloted, so no duration is suggested), no code except in Part C, no computer algebra in Part B; a scientific calculator (or Python used only as a calculator) is allowed for the arithmetic in Part B, since several questions need cube roots, a 3 × 3 solve by hand, an exponential and a few square roots. There is no enforced lock; the point is to find out what you can do unaided. Solutions and pass thresholds are in `solutions/phase-05/exam/`.

Report your result per competency: (1) mathematical and physical reasoning, (2) analytical problem solving, (3) computational competence, (4) engineering interpretation. Questions are tagged with the competencies they assess. Every question is written over an aerospace structure; the mechanics is what is being tested, and the framing is there so that you also practise deciding which model applies and what it assumes. The organising idea of the phase is *equilibrium, compatibility and constitutive law*: in every question draw the free-body diagram first, say which of the three the step uses, say what the model assumes and where it fails, and check the main result by an independent route (a second method, a limiting case, or a different cut). SI units throughout. Sign conventions unless a question says otherwise: tension positive; for beams x along the span from the left end or the root, y up, shear force V positive when it acts downward on the right-hand face of a cut, bending moment M positive when it causes sagging (compression on top); stress components σ_x, σ_y, τ_xy with τ_xy positive on the positive x face in the positive y direction. Use g = 9.81 m/s².

## Part A: Concepts

**1.** (1, 4) Free-body diagrams and determinacy. (a) A main landing-gear leg is pinned to the wing at its top and held by a drag brace pinned at both ends. Draw the free-body diagram of the leg alone during a braked landing: name every force with the body that exerts it, and say why the drag brace may be drawn as a single force along its own axis while the pin at the top must be drawn as two unknown components. State the general condition that makes a member a two-force member, and give one aircraft member that looks like a strut but is *not* one. (b) Write the equations of equilibrium for a rigid body in 2-D and in 3-D, say how many independent scalar equations each gives, and state the counting test for static determinacy of a planar truss (m + r against 2j). Say why passing the count is necessary but not sufficient, with an example of a support arrangement that passes the count and is still a mechanism. (c) An aircraft standing on a nose wheel and two main wheels is statically determinate for vertical loads; a four-wheel trolley is not. Say what extra information an indeterminate problem needs (name the two ingredients besides equilibrium) and why the answer then depends on the stiffness of the parts and the flatness of the floor. (d) A learner adds a "force of the brace" *and* "the brace's reaction" to the same free-body diagram of the leg. Say what is wrong, using Newton's third law and the definition of a system boundary.

**2.** (1) Stress, strain and the constitutive law. (a) Define normal and shear stress at a point as limits of force per area on a cut, and say why the stress at a point needs a tensor (how many components in 3-D, and why only six are independent; which equilibrium argument gives the symmetry τ_xy = τ_yx). (b) Define normal and engineering shear strain from displacements and say which of the three ingredients (equilibrium, compatibility, constitutive law) each of these definitions belongs to. State Hooke's law for an isotropic material in 3-D for ε_x and the relation G = E/(2(1 + ν)). Say what Poisson's ratio is physically and why ν = 0.5 means incompressible. (c) Say what Saint-Venant's principle states and why it lets you use σ = P/A in the middle of a bar but not next to a bolt hole; give the stress concentration factor of a small circular hole in a wide plate under uniaxial tension and say why a fatigue crack in a fuselage starts at a rivet hole. (d) Say what "linear elastic" assumes and name two situations in this phase where it fails (one material, one geometric), each with the quantity you would check to know you are outside it.

**3.** (1, 4) Failure and margins. (a) Define limit load and ultimate load, say what the factors of safety 1.0 (limit to yield) and 1.5 (limit to ultimate) each protect against, and write the margin of safety. Say what a margin of +0.00 means and why a large positive margin on a primary structure is also a finding to report. (b) State the Tresca and von Mises criteria for a general 3-D stress state in terms of principal stresses, say what physical idea each one comes from, and give the ratio of their predictions in pure shear. Say which is more conservative and by how much at most. (c) Say why neither criterion is used for a cast or brittle part, state the brittle Mohr–Coulomb criterion for σ₁ > 0 > σ₃, and say what it predicts for pure shear when the compressive strength is four times the tensile strength. (d) A slender strut can fail with every stress far below yield. Name the failure mode, say why a stress criterion cannot predict it, and say which of the three ingredients (equilibrium, compatibility, constitutive law) is applied differently in a buckling analysis than in every other analysis of the phase.

**4.** (1) Beam theory and energy methods. (a) State the assumptions of Euler–Bernoulli beam theory (plane sections, small slopes, linear elasticity, the load plane) and derive, in words and one line of algebra each, how they lead to σ = −My/I and to EI v'' = M. Say how the dimensionless ratio (depth/length)² decides when shear deformation matters, and give its order of magnitude for a wing spar and for a short, deep fitting. (b) State the differential relations between load, shear and moment, dV/dx = −w and dM/dx = V, derive them from a slice of beam, and say what each implies about the V and M diagrams at a point load, at a concentrated couple and at a point where V = 0. (c) State Castigliano's second theorem, say what kind of structure it requires, and say how a "dummy load" gives a deflection where no load acts. State the unit-load (virtual work) method and say why the two give the same integral for a linear structure. (d) State Maxwell–Betti reciprocity in words and give one test you could do on a wing with two dial gauges and two masses that would check it.
